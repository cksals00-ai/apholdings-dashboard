## 1. Meta Muse — arrived with 15 UK stores named, settled into Instagram DMs, and the banks stand on the other side (facts, company announcements and media)

We read Meta Newsroom's two posts (November 23) directly. **“Introducing Muse in the UK”** is the launch announcement. Meta's personal agent Muse (NO.21, 22, 43, 53, 58) opened to UK users aged 18 and over on iOS, Android, muse.ai and WhatsApp, with Meta AI glasses “in the coming months”. Most features are free, with an optional subscription for heavier use, but no price is given. The underlying model is Muse Spark (in the company's words, “the first model from Meta Superintelligence Labs”). Partners are named — Spotify and OpenTable globally, and **15 in the UK**: Marks & Spencer, Debenhams Group, Currys, Ocado Retail, Trainline, Argos, Sainsbury's, ASOS, Jet2, LOOKFANTASTIC, MyProtein, Cult Beauty, JustPark, easyJet and JD Sports, with PayPal “coming soon”. Payment comes in two forms — **Onelink**, built by Stripe (a virtual card dedicated to agents), and Shopify's **Shop Pay** at supported stores (company description). There are no figures such as user numbers, only the phrase “millions of Britons” from Meta's UK country director (we leave it as a phrase, not a figure).

**“Muse Does the Work, You Stay in Control”** is the post explaining controls and privacy (company claims). Users choose which apps to connect and the scope of permissions, can put expiry dates on permissions, are asked for approval before actions such as sending email or making a purchase; a separate “security agent” checks Muse's actions against the permissions granted; users can see the full activity log, watch web tasks and step in, and view and delete memories. **Muse cannot see passwords or payment methods, and it ignores verification codes and password-reset links**, the post says (this is the exact spot where NO.36 wrote about “when a verification code reaches an agent's hands”). Conversations and stored information are not shared with Meta's ad systems; **use for AI training is on by default at sign-up and can be turned off in settings**; private messages such as email and Instagram DMs are not used for training. Instagram, Facebook and Threads connect automatically when they share the same Accounts Center. Neither of the two UK posts says a word about banks or finance.

We read TechCrunch (Aisha Malik, November 24) directly. Muse converses and acts **inside Instagram DM threads** — you can share a post and say something like “find me a Black Friday deal on these shoes”, catch up on missed DMs, or turn saved recipe Reels into a shopping list, travel content into an itinerary, and fashion Reels into outfit suggestions. From November 24 on iOS and Android in the **US, Canada, Mexico, Japan and Singapore**, with the UK left out; the saved-content organizing guide is US and Canada only (TechCrunch's account). Meta's explanation includes “it does not post or send without approval, and forgetting and undo are available”. The relationship to advertising is not in this article — in NO.58 we cited the CNBC figure relayed by TNW (51% of agent orders came from Muse), and we still could not open the CNBC original this week (CNBC's tech section returned client_error for the fourth time). The talks with Amazon about lifting the block (Fortune, November 25, anonymous sources, no comment from either company) were relayed by TechCrunch (Lauren Forristal, November 25) as “the sticking points are search-result placement and ad handling, and both CEOs must sign off” — as written in NO.58, with nothing newly confirmed.

**The other side.** We read The Paypers (Claudia Pincovski, November 10) directly. “Reportedly” is right there in the headline — HSBC, Nationwide, NatWest, Barclays and Lloyds have decided to preemptively block AI agents from accessing customer accounts, some including a ban on transactions on customers' behalf, and there is no sign yet of any agent having accessed an account. Two reasons — there are no rules on who is liable when an agent mishandles money, and the effect that automated large-scale account movements could have on revenue and financial stability (citing a McKinsey estimate that “up to £70 billion could move”). The article singles out Muse (then due to launch in the UK), and distinguishes Anthropic's and OpenAI's personal finance products as “view only, no actions”. There are no bank spokesperson statements; the Lloyds CFO's “we have not seen signs but we are preparing” and the Standard Chartered CFO's “it has to be safe for both customers and banks” are quoted. The article does not say which outlet first reported it (a secondary aggregator site said The Telegraph, November 8, but we could not open that original). On the US side we read American Banker (Penny Crosman, October 27) directly — JPMorgan, Citi, Bank of America, Wells Fargo and Morgan Stanley are blocking external agents, and at an unnamed large bank an ordinary-looking agent **reset a password, changed the approved transfer account and withdrew $100,000**, a case the fraud team reported to the CEO with a recommendation for a full block (source on condition the bank not be named). The back half of the article is paywalled and we could not read it. This is the original of the case we noted in NO.58 via Money Today.

## 2. Cloudflare's “Search for Agents” — the switch that lets a store hand agents a search box, and “robots.txt is not a permission” (facts, company announcement, industry body and media)

We read Cloudflare's official blog post “Search for Agents” (November 25, co-written by three engineers) and the developer docs (updated the same day) directly. When a site owner flips one toggle in the domain Overview on the dashboard (also possible via API), Cloudflare's AI Search automatically creates an instance that crawls and indexes the site and opens search at the site domain's **`/.well-known/search?q=…`**. Four meta tags go into the HTML head so agents can find this search box — `agent:instructions`, `agent:endpoint` (the search URL template), `agent:method` (GET) and `agent:documentation`. If the earlier “Markdown for Agents” is on, the same information comes as YAML front matter in response to an `Accept: text/markdown` request. The HTTP QUERY method is also accepted (the blog describes it as IETF Proposed Standard RFC 10008 from June 2026 — Cloudflare is a co-author; we did not open the RFC). **The response is markdown search results, not a generated answer** — each document comes with a title, a relevance score (like 1.000 or 0.951) and an excerpt, and the answer is written by the model of the agent that called. The company gives two reasons — until now the owner had to do manual setup for an agent to know a site, and instead of scraping pages one by one the agent gets the relevant content via search, “without a second AI model answering on its behalf”.

Scope and price — the blog says “all customers, including the Free plan, with limits”, while the docs say **“beta on all plans”**, so the two documents differ in wording. Billing is said only to follow standard AI Search pricing (ingestion, index storage, queries), with the numbers deferred to the pricing page. The figures PPC Land (Luis Rijo, November 26) transcribed from the pricing page (updated November 17) are — $0.75 per million tokens of index input (5 million tokens per month included), $2 per GB-month of storage (10 GB included), $0.75 per 1,000 hybrid or semantic searches (1,000 per month included), $0.10 per 1,000 keyword searches, billing starting December 1 — and “Workers Free crawls 500 pages a day” appears only in PPC Land and not on the docs page we read (one outlet). The docs' defaults are 100,000 pages, a link depth of 5 hops, a 24-hour sync, hybrid search, up to 10 result chunks and reranking off. **Access control** — search requests are also subject to that domain's bot management, AI Crawl Control (the tool for allowing, charging or blocking AI crawlers) and WAF rules. robots.txt appears nowhere in the blog or the docs (PPC Land likewise states “this article does not describe robots.txt handling”). The August AI Search post did say the crawler follows robots.txt and that the user-agent name is `Cloudflare-AI-Search` (we saw this in the summary toward the end of that post, so treat it as a caveat). The same instance's MCP endpoint is said to be separate from the search address. There are no adoption figures.

A day later, **IAB Europe** (the European digital advertising industry body) released the “Publisher Playbook for the Agentic Web” (November 26). We read the announcement page directly — an interactive tool made by its Publisher Content & AI Taskforce and AI Working Group, with feedback from the German BVDW working group, consisting of: the revenue lines exposed as the user journey shifts from “browse→ask→delegate”; a self-assessment of the **7 layers** where value leaks out of the publisher; classification of automated traffic and access rules by AI use (from model training to agent action); a procedure for checking side effects before blocking, so that verification and measurement partners are not blocked too; the distinction between GEO (being found by AI) and licensing (being paid for content); **6 licensing models and 13 pre-contract due-diligence questions**; requirements for an AI advertising environment; and a 90-day action plan. It says of itself that it recommends no prices, contract terms or single strategy. PPC Land (same day) rendered the self-assessment as “12 areas scored 0–3 (36 points maximum)”, the access rules as “by 7 use types”, and the 90 days as “three 30-day phases with 21 actions (days 1–30: owner, baseline, rights gaps, mapping robots/WAF/paywall controls; days 31–60: posture by use type, product design, contract questionnaire; days 61–90: one reversible pilot)” — the announcement's “7 layers” and PPC Land's “12 areas” may be different levels of the same tool, but we did not open the interactive tool itself, so we do not settle it. And the sentence closest to this article's headline — **“robots.txt states a preference, it is not an access permission; enforcement requires WAF/CDN rules or authentication”** — is a playbook sentence as relayed by PPC Land; the word robots.txt does not appear in the body of the IAB announcement (so we leave it as a one-outlet relay).

## 3. The scoreboard — everything fell when the grading bar was raised, four agents together are 1.3x faster but use 3x the tokens, and OpenAI measured its own “46% of 2–4 hour tasks” (facts, independent evaluator and company announcement)

We read two pages from the independent evaluator Vals AI directly. **Vibe Code Bench v1.1.1** — the methodology section says “published November 23” while the top of the leaderboard says “Updated 11/21”, so the dates differ. What changed is the grading (Vals' account) — the browser testing agent that actually clicks through the apps was swapped for a newer model and a newer Browser Use version; a second judge model re-reviews failure verdicts and does not count a failure that has no evidence in the logs; **the pass criterion was tightened from “90% of sub-steps succeed” to “all sub-steps succeed”**; UI test steps that were impossible or open to multiple interpretations were fixed or removed; tasks and specs stayed the same; the apps the models built were not rebuilt but **only re-graded**. Evaluation cost is said to have fallen 85–90%. The result is one sentence — “scores decreased across the board”. How much they fell, old score→new score, is not on the page. The leaderboard has 51 models, and the top five (OpenHands harness, score / cost per test) are **Claude Fable 5.2 83.52% / $22.13, Claude Sonnet 5.5 80.88% / $19.73, Gemini 4 Neon 80.23% / $6.76, GPT-6.2 Sol 79.48% / $3.23, Claude Fable 5.1 78.73% / $33.37**. Since models from the maker of the AI that wrote this article are three of the five, we only transcribe the numbers and write no sentence about “which model is better” — the only thing to note is the shape of the table itself: a 4-point score gap between 1st and 4th, but close to a 7x difference in cost per test (NO.46's “cost per task” column).

**Swarm Speed** (“Updated 11/23”) — a new benchmark measuring “are teams of agents more efficient”. For each of 76 tasks, it runs one single-agent run and one run by a team of 4 of the same model; the speed multiple is the ratio of the time the two setups take to reach 95% of the same reference score, and tokens are the ratio of the team's total tokens to the single agent's (Vals' account). Results — the team gets there **1.2–1.3x faster** (about 20% less time), 1.3–1.8x on software engineering tasks, **about 3x the tokens** (3.2x in the example), 33–34% in efficiency terms, and the final score within 3 percentage points of the single agent. The list of which models were tested is not in the text (only “for each model tested”), and it is introduced as “a dynamic benchmark with a rotating set of tasks”. NO.57 wrote about “the approve button in the agent's hands”; this is the first time we have seen in numbers whether, when there are four of those hands, cost rises as much as speed does.

**OpenAI, “Research acceleration: The jagged frontier”** (November 18) — we read the official post directly (we could not open the collapsed “Methods” tab). It is OpenAI's own measurement of coding-agent use **inside its own research organization**. On tasks estimated to take a human 2–4 hours, the “estimated success rate without human intervention” rose from 23% in March to 46% in October, and on 4–8 hour tasks from 13% to 35%. By task type (August 1 to October 18): monitoring training runs 79%, answering technical questions 72%, launching training runs 35%, building evaluation environments 29%, **writing inference and serving stack code 25%** — this variance is the “jagged frontier” of the title. Daily agent cost per researcher, at list API prices, was a median of $2,400 (October, up 62% from September) and $23,000 for the top 10% (up 105%). There is also a survey median (101 researchers, late August to early September): they would “give up 40% of the team's headcount or 30% of the team's compute to get the latest coding agents back three months sooner”. The post adds its own caveats — the survey sample is “ad-hoc”, the change in success rates may mix “capability improvements with changes in what researchers choose to delegate”, and the report “should not be read as an intention to pursue full self-improvement”. All of it is company-published self-measurement with no external audit. PPC Land (November 23) headlined it as “46% of long tasks”, but the original says “2–4 hour tasks”, so we do not use that phrasing.

## Reading the three together (interpretation)

First, **store names have started appearing in agent announcements.** In Australia (NO.53) it was nine connectors, in Canada one, Loblaw; in the UK it was fifteen named stores plus two payment methods (an agent-only virtual card and Shop Pay). NO.55 wrote “a store has four doors to decide on” and NO.58 “ask about identity first”; this is the first week the list of stores that chose to open those doors went into the platform's announcement. Five banks in the same country are, “reportedly”, on the list on the other side. The difference between the two lists is not industry but **the size of the box the money moves in** — retail is “one approval, one purchase”, banking is “moving accounts”. And Meta's sentence “the agent cannot see passwords or payment methods, and ignores verification codes” is a platform answering by design the risk we wrote about in NO.36 (“don't hand over your inbox”), but whether that answer actually holds there is still no way to check beyond the company's claim. “Training use on by default” came out of two companies (Meta and Google) in the same week in the same shape — being able to turn it off is not the same as it being off.

Second, **“blocking” and “handing over a search box” are different switches.** NO.37 described how to write “do not use for training” in one line of robots.txt. This week two documents stated the limits of that line in the same words — the IAB playbook (via PPC Land): “robots.txt is a preference, not a permission; enforcement needs WAF/CDN or authentication”; Cloudflare: even the search endpoint “is subject to bot management, AI Crawl Control and WAF”. So a store's agent door has three layers — **preference (robots.txt), enforcement (WAF/CDN rules) and invitation (a search box such as /.well-known/search)**. Our articles so far have covered only the first two; the third layer came into being this week. The distinguishing feature of the invitation layer is that “the agent takes only excerpts instead of scraping whole pages”, but whether those excerpts are used for training or only cited in answers depends not on the search box but on the rules of the agent taking them — which is probably also why there is no robots.txt in Cloudflare's docs. Turning this layer on comes after deciding “who gets in” (NO.58).

Third, **a score is a function of the grading sheet, and “fast” and “cheap” are different columns.** Vals only re-graded the same apps, and said all 51 scores went down, without saying by how much. To NO.21's “the arithmetic first”, NO.52's “model name + tier” and NO.53's “+ footnote + date”, this week adds **“+ grading-sheet version”** — even under the same benchmark name, v1.1 and v1.1.1 are different tables. Swarm Speed's “1.3x faster, 3x the tokens” means a “number of agents” column is needed between NO.46's cost-per-task column and NO.57's tool-call-count column, and OpenAI's 46% is a number the company measured itself inside its own lab, so it cannot be placed alongside outside numbers (the post says as much itself). Because models from the maker of the AI that wrote this article sit at the top of the leaderboard in this section, we add no judgment — we noted only the table's shape (a 4-point gap at 7x the cost).

## What we would do — three things for a small team running commerce, an app and an AI influencer this week (practice)

1. **Write our site's “three layers of the agent door” on one page — preference, enforcement, invitation.** For each of apholdings.kr, Hangul Cups and CourtMate, one line each: NO.37's robots.txt (preference), the bot rules of the CDN and hosting we use (enforcement), and “will we hand agents a search box” (invitation). We do not open the invitation layer yet — first the CEO decides “may agents take excerpts of AP News articles (citation in answers is welcome; training follows the NO.37 standard)”, and once that is decided, whether our CDN is Cloudflare or not, we only write down in the document the names of the four meta tags (`agent:instructions`, `endpoint`, `method`, `documentation`) and `/.well-known/search` (we do not imitate and build it ourselves). The CourtMate board is an invitation-layer candidate only up to NO.58's “public information — read”; member posts are excluded.
2. **Read the 15-store UK list backwards as “what it would take for us to be on the list”.** For a store to get onto the partner list of an agent like Muse, four things can be worked back from the announcement — product data an agent can read (NO.50's “one page per store”), a payment path such as an agent-only virtual card or Shop Pay, an order-confirmation flow that meshes with the pre-purchase approval step, and identity verification of the incoming agent (NO.58). This week Hangul Cups sends a one-line question to our PG provider — **“does our PG accept agent-only virtual cards (like Onelink)?”** — and writes the answer in the “payment” box of NO.55's door table. If it cannot, our default is “payment by a human — agents only as far as the cart”, and that too just needs writing down. The five banks on the other side are the same signal to us: keep the “member information — write” box closed.
3. **Add “grading-sheet version” and “number of agents” columns to the cost-per-task table, and try changing the grading sheet once.** We add two columns to the table from NO.46, 52, 57 and 58 (model / tier / completed / tokens / tool calls / snippets inserted / cost per task / time). Then we copy what Vals did, in miniature — for our 3 tasks (extracting ingredient-list items, classifying commerce inquiries, proofreading Lia's scripts), we **change the pass criterion from “90% of items correct passes” to “all items must be correct” and only re-grade past results**, and write down how far the scores drop. We do not re-run, so the cost is zero. We do not run a 4-agent team — our tasks have no reason yet to spend 3x the tokens, and if we did run one, we would first pick a single “task where time needs to come down”. The results go in the next article, and if the scores do not drop, we write “they did not drop”. For Lia's script, “the biggest social company launched an agent with the names of 15 UK stores written in, and five banks in the same country were reported to have closed their doors” goes in in a learner's tone, with the figures only as reported.
