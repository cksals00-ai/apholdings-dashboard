## 1. Poppy 1.0 — the store writes "what an agent may do in our house" in a single file (fact, company announcement)

Sierra (the AI customer-service company led by former Salesforce co-CEO Bret Taylor) released **"Personal Agent Protocol" — nicknamed Poppy — version 1.0** on its official blog on November 13 (co-written by Bret Taylor and Clay Bavor). It first announced the protocol with Meta on October 6, published a draft on October 9, and wrote that it "tested the drafts against real websites, APIs and agents for five weeks." We read the specification itself (personalagentprotocol.org). This is how it works — **a company places one JSON document at `/.well-known/poppy.json` on its own domain.** Inside are `organization` (name, domain, logo), the doors an agent may enter (`agent.protocols` conversation endpoints, `apis` — OpenAPI or MCP, `web` — a browser-session endpoint, `tasks`, `extensions`), and policies (`auth.disabled` to turn off sign-in, `web.disabled` "do not browse our website," `web.unscoped` whether browser sign-in means full account access). There is a `scope_hints` field that marks each scope as **`irreversible` (an action that cannot be undone) or `personal_data` (involving personal data).** Sign-in uses OAuth 2.0 (PKCE, device sign-in RFC 8628) and sessions use a signed `Poppy-Session` token. The spec describes itself as "1.0 is 0.4 plus fixes" and hands areas not yet covered to a roadmap page. Sierra's post says Poppy sits on existing standards such as MCP, OpenAPI and UCP, that the commerce extension was co-authored by Shopify, and that there is an implementation kit with a validator.

Participation figures are the company's. To October's eight initial design partners (Meta, Genesys, Instinct, NiCE, Rocket, Shopify, Stripe, Walmart) sixty more were added for **more than 100** (including Affirm, Delta Air Lines, Salesforce, Twilio, Wayfair and Zoom), and those actually live are Mercury (a bank — payment requests go through the account's approval policy), Synchrony and Turkish Airlines (loyalty members approve a business-class upgrade before payment). Two sentences are the center of this article. **"Every Shopify store now automatically publishes a Poppy file, so merchants don't need to do any extra implementation"** (Sierra) — Shopify powers stores in more than 175 countries; UCP handles cart and checkout, while Poppy handles "who are you and what may you do" before that. And **"Meta's Muse automatically looks for Poppy first on every site it visits."** TNW (November 13) put Muse's users at "more than 10 million" (one outlet), and added that despite the 1.0 number the spec is labelled a draft and may change in ways that break compatibility, that Visa and Mastercard are building separate agent-identity checks, and that Amazon wants shoppers to use its own agent instead of Muse.

Two lines of background. According to Practical Ecommerce (September 24), on September 8 — the day Muse launched — Shopify added Meta to "Agentic Storefronts," sharing merchant products with Meta by default (merchants can turn it off), while Amazon blocked the same Muse with a message that "continued access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use" (NO.43). The words of Sierra's co-founders relayed by CMSWire (October 7) are **"consumers decide what access to give their personal agents, and companies set limits on what those agents can do,"** and Meta called it "a standard that, like email, no single party owns," but no governance model was published and the two companies' partner lists differed. The spec site's footer states **Apache License 2.0** (personalagentprotocol.org, checked directly on November 18); no standards body or governing entity is named. Payment is not yet core but a "candidate extension."

## 2. Uber — "identity only answers 'who'"; each agent's permitted intents and risk tier are registered, then inspected at the moment of execution (fact, company announcement)

We read the Uber Engineering blog post "Securing AI Agents: Lessons From Real-World Deployment" (November 6, by Sean Tout, Max Zhurovich and Adi Raghavendra) directly, and InfoQ (November 15) summarized it. The starting sentence is this — **"Traditional authorization can determine who is allowed to act, but it can't determine whether an agent's proposed action is appropriate in context."** What it tries to stop is prompt injection, tool and memory poisoning, goal hijacking, behavioral drift and hallucination-driven actions (methods are not in the post, and we do not write them either). The structure has three layers. **① Before deployment**: every agent is registered in the "AI Registry" with identity, owner, persona, **permitted intents, constraints, dependencies and risk tier**, and an automated analyzer called "Argus" scans code, prompts, configurations, dependencies, endpoints and tool-invocation paths for weaknesses and pre-generates persona and intent drafts for developers to review (MCP servers and tools go through the same process). **② At runtime**: "AI Guard" is the policy decision point, running on **small specialized models hosted locally**, and "Agent Guard" hooks intercept actions at the boundaries of agent and tool execution and apply the verdict. What is checked is whether the action fits the persona, permitted intents and semantic constraints, whether it suits the user's request and role, and whether it makes sense given the whole session. **③ After deployment**: an execution trace that links agent actions, tool calls and decisions in one line (sensitive data redacted, content included only when needed), and a device that **"replays"** recorded interactions against new policies before they are enforced.

The difficulties are written down too — **the balance between false blocks and false allows** (handled by shadow mode that only records without blocking, replay, and per-agent calibration), latency (parallel execution, small specialized models instead of large general ones), developer friction (Argus automation), and the fact that "the same rules for every agent" did not fit agents with different purposes and risks. The post closes with **"security, accuracy, latency, cost, agent autonomy and developer experience can't be optimized independently"** and promises follow-up posts (persona, context-aware guardrails, observability, intent filtering). **There is not a single figure** — no false-block rate, latency, cost or agent count. Nor does it say whether anything is open source. For reference, there is a report (Techstrong, August 10) that Uber released a separate tool called "ADR" in August — more than 50,000 agents, ten months in operation, "credential exposure was more common than prompt injection," all company figures — but neither that article nor the November post mentions the other, so **we could not confirm the relationship between the two.** We relay one reader's question from the InfoQ article as is — "the open question is how this works for agents you don't build."

## 3. Apple — attaching "clinical citations, detection of concerning questions, no training use" to health AI answers and asking 200,000 people to rate them (fact, company announcement)

On November 15 Apple announced the **"Apple Health AI Usage Study"** in its Newsroom and the same day put the "Apple Health AI Study App" (developer Apple Inc., free, iPhone only, iOS 26.0 or later) on the US App Store. We read both pages. The collaborator is **OpenEvidence** — a Miami company founded in 2022 that runs an LLM medical search engine for physicians; according to Wikipedia, as of December 2025 about 760,000 US physicians were registered and it was used for 18 million clinical consultations a month (company figures), and it holds content licenses with NEJM and JAMA (we do not cover its fundraising in this article). Eligibility is **US residents aged 21 or over, an iPhone 15 Pro or later with Apple Intelligence enabled, iOS 26 or later, up to 200,000 participants.** Participants ask everyday health questions, read complex medical information in plain language, and rate the quality of answers in surveys. Answers **draw on medical sources such as NEJM, JAMA and Nature, and each answer carries links to peer-reviewed clinical citations.** Those who wish can share resting heart rate, activity, sleep and lab results from the Health app to see how much personal data changes the relevance of answers. Three conditions are written in the post — **"study data will not be used to train AI models,"** the app has guardrails **"intended to detect certain concerning questions and suggest that a participant follow up with a provider or other resources,"** and **"the app is not meant to diagnose or treat any condition, or replace the advice of medical professionals."** Sumbul Desai, M.D., Apple's VP of Health and Fitness, said "health is deeply personal" and called the study "the most thoughtful way to understand how people engage with, comprehend, and evaluate health AI." The study's duration is not in the Newsroom post.

What the media added comes one item per outlet. TechCrunch (November 15) wrote that the study runs **more than 12 months**, that Health data is anonymized before going to "a secure third-party cloud provider," and that **OpenEvidence deletes query and response data after 24 hours** (these three are not in Apple's Newsroom post, so TechCrunch alone). The same article added that OpenAI said earlier this year it receives "more than 300 million health-related queries per week" (OpenAI's original is not reachable from our side, unconfirmed). TechCrunch gave the device requirement as "iPhone 15 or later," while Apple's Newsroom and Business Standard (November 16) say "iPhone 15 Pro or later," so we followed Apple's wording. iGeeksBlog (November 16) quoted Apple as writing that LLM-based consumer health tools "may at times offer responses that are incomplete or unclear," but we could not find that sentence in the Newsroom text we read, so we leave it as one outlet. The App Store privacy label read "User ID — linked to you (analytics, app functionality)," with an age rating of 18+.

## Reading the three together (interpretation)

First, **all three are documents written "before the action."** The "stop button" of NO.39 is pressed after the action, and AT&T's "accuracy in the mid-to-high 90s" in NO.49 is a threshold measured while acting. These three come before that — before the agent arrives (poppy.json), before the agent is run (registry), before the answer goes out (citation, detection, notice). The order moving from after to before looks like the direction of the second half of this year (interpretation). The common field across the three documents is **"actions that cannot be undone"** — Poppy marks a scope `irreversible`, Uber speaks of an evidence chain that "ties privileged actions back to what the user actually requested," and Apple hands concerning questions to a person (a provider). In NO.39 we wrote that "irreversible actions belong outside the agent's hands from the start"; that sentence has become a field name in a protocol.

Second, **"blocking" and "opening" are written in the same file.** In poppy.json, "do not browse our website" (`web.disabled`) and "come in through the API" (`apis`) sit side by side. It means United's ban (NO.45) and Shopify's opening can become sentences of the same form, so the customer's agent reads not "blocked" but "this door is closed and that one is open" (interpretation). Three cautions when reading, though — the spec is "1.0 but a draft" with breaking changes announced, the license is stated as Apache License 2.0 but no governing entity is named, and the question CMSWire posed, "which company's agent guards the store's door" (Sierra itself sells customer-service agents), is open. "More than 100" is the count of design partners while those that have "turned it on" are three, so when relaying the numbers we keep two separate columns (NO.21).

Third, **Uber and Apple are not "documents for removing people" but "documents that write the conditions for handing over to a person."** Uber's registry looks like paperwork for granting autonomy, but the core is "block an action that does not fit the context just before execution" and "run new rules first in a shadow mode that only records." Apple has 200,000 people rate answers while first writing "not diagnosis or treatment" and "concerning questions go to a provider." In NO.03 we described how we made our ingredient-label app not say "safe"; Apple's document is the large edition of that — the differences are that **every answer carries a citation link and the person rating the answer is the user** (interpretation). The absence of figures is shared too — Uber did not give a false-block rate, Apple a duration, Sierra an actual session count. Not one number in this article was counted outside the company.

## If it were us — three things for a team running commerce, apps and an AI influencer to do this week (practice)

1. **Fill in `/.well-known/poppy.json` for our commerce domain first "without publishing it."** Moving NO.43's "three lines of rules for incoming agents" and NO.45's "why" column into this file's fields shows the blanks at once — `organization`, which doors to open (`web` only or `apis` too), whether to turn on `web.disabled`, whether to mark orders and payments `irreversible` and shipping addresses `personal_data` in `scope_hints`. Because the spec is a draft, **publishing comes after the CEO's decision**, and we write "1.0 = draft, breaking changes possible" at the top of the table. If any external channel carrying our products runs on Shopify, that store already has the file auto-published, so we read once what is written there.
2. **Write our own agent registry on one page with Uber's seven columns.** Lia script generation, ingredient-label judgment (NO.03), commerce inquiry replies, and this very session that collects and writes AP News — for each, identity, owner, persona, permitted intents, constraints, dependencies (which MCP servers and tools it uses) and risk tier. It goes next to the "threshold N% for removing the human" column of NO.49. When adding a new constraint, run it like Uber in **shadow mode for two weeks** (record only, no blocking), then look at the false-block and false-allow counts before switching it on.
3. **Attach Apple's three lines to the answers of SafeList (our ingredient-label app).** ① an evidence link on every answer (for us, the MFDS notices and the raw-material database are the citations), ② a list of "concerning questions" (allergic reactions, interactions with medicines, consumption by infants and the like get "ask a professional" instead of an answer), ③ whether the terms actually contain the sentence "user questions are not used to train models" and a retention period as a number (like the "24 hours" TechCrunch reported). And one rating button under each answer ("correct / not sure / wrong") — not 200,000 people, but we start counting from 20.
