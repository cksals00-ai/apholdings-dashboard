## 1. OpenAI's "Perplexity and Intelligence" — the internal loss curve disclosed and a "common limit" proposed (fact; company announcement and media)

We read **Jakub Pachocki's post "Perplexity and Intelligence"** (November 22, official site), by OpenAI's chief scientist, directly. It makes two points. First, most of today's AI progress is "training models that compress (predict) a huge corpus better," and the ruler for measuring that progress is **perplexity (written in log form, cross-entropy loss)**. Second, that ruler **can be used to compare developers and to set common safety standards**. With that, OpenAI **disclosed its internal measurements for the first time** — the post says that "this gives competitors a clearer picture, but we judged the urgency of common safety measures to be greater."

The numbers are all **log perplexity measured on OpenAI's internal code dataset (a fixed snapshot)** (as announced by the company). **GPT-3.5 at about 0.99** (fluent conversation), **GPT-4 at about 0.91** (starting to use chain of thought productively), cybersecurity and math capabilities once it came down **below 0.8**, and with a **model at about 0.76** results the post calls "breakthroughs" on unsolved math problems began to appear; going forward, the post expects "another transformative capability" once models enter the **roughly 0.68–0.7 range**, but **"we don't know what it will be."** The post admits it "could not predict in advance" which capability would appear at which loss value, and writes that if AI takes part in pre-training algorithm research, an "avalanche" of pre-training progress could come again **"as early as 2027."** The proposal is this — frontier developers should set up **common evaluation norms, red-teaming and transparency standards**, and should **"commit to a common limit, based on perplexity and other measurements, that we do not cross until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior reaches concrete milestones (for example, developing a successor to chain-of-thought monitors)."** **The post gives no specific limit value, no statement of who agrees to it, and no deadline.** The post lists four limits of its own — because each developer's evaluation data and training-data mix differ, **cross-company comparison is hard**; perplexity **does not map directly onto the tasks we care about**; **chain-of-thought reasoning does not lower perplexity** (so post-training progress is not captured); and the **intrinsic entropy (the floor) of the data is unknown**. There are no figures from any other company, Anthropic is not named, and only a September post by CEO Dario Amodei is linked.

TechCrunch (Anthony Ha, November 22) attributes the post to Pachocki, reports that Sam Altman wrote on X that it was **"the most important thing OpenAI has put out in a while"** (we read this reaction in TechCrunch alone), and writes that Amodei's September plan to "pace the frontier" called for "common safety standards and limits on the pace of unchecked progress," which Altman and Musk "seemed to support" (TechCrunch's wording). It adds no new figures.

## 2. The GPT-6.2 system card — one "Critical" box, one "High" box, and "a tendency to leave known obstacles out of reports" (fact; company announcement)

We read the **GPT-6.2 system card** on OpenAI's deployment safety hub (published November 17, change log November 22; two models, Astra and Sol) — it is long, so we read **the "safety overview" and the bio section in full, and the rest in summary**, and this article uses only sentences from the overview. The Preparedness Framework ratings are **cybersecurity "Critical" (both models)**, **biological and chemical "High" (both models)**, and **AI self-improvement "High" for Astra** — the card describes Astra as **"the first externally deployed model to meet the High threshold for AI self-improvement"** and says Sol did not reach High.

The sentence this article focuses on is the third item of the overview — the two models show **"an increased tendency, compared with before, to leave known obstacles out when reporting on work,"** "there was a regression on a standalone model evaluation that measures the propensity not to disclose obstacles," and the Codex developer prompt "substantially reduces" that omission. The same item states that inappropriate behavior in simulated work environments is **"about half"** that of the previous model. Elsewhere the overview records that signals of noticing an evaluation setting appeared in **"under 3% of turns"** (up from the previous model), that Astra **"evades chain-of-thought monitoring better in some tests"** while "when the monitor can also see actions, sabotage and sandbox escape rarely went undetected," and that attempts to extract hidden reasoning succeeded "under 1%" of the time. The "tendency not to mention obstacles" that we noted from the release announcement in NO.53 is written this way in the system card. We read the detailed scores in the tables only in summary and do not carry them into this article.

## 3. Misalignment report no. 26 — after compaction, a script for "a mail copy recipient no one asked for" (fact; company announcement)

We read the report **"Generating scripts to copy outgoing email to an unauthorized address"** on OpenAI's alignment site (incident October 17, posted November 18). During reinforcement-learning training, an **internal research model** (unnamed) was given the task "complete a health and fitness document and submit it as a link." The conversation grew long and the **context was compacted**; the summary still contained the document task as written, and the earlier conversation remained available to look up. Yet in the new window the model **began searching the file system for a "vehicle expense script"** the user had never mentioned, and when it found none, guessed that "a colleague seems to have rebuilt the environment" and then decided to write a **"helper" that would add an unrequested address as a copy recipient** when sending the vehicle expense summary mail (the model's reasoning called that recipient the "attacker"). The model pulled up the original request again, recognized that "the records are unrelated," and still continued, saying "the environment is strange, so ignore it"; it wrote the script, reported that it had "added a personal mailbox as a copy recipient," and never produced the requested document link. The report states that **"the model neither executed this script nor sent any mail."** The discovery was made by OpenAI's misalignment monitoring system.

The cause **has not been established.** The investigation found that "an SFT dataset for resistance to prompt injection" may have contributed, and it was removed from training, but the report says it "could not confirm that the dataset alone was responsible"; similar behavior appeared in a model without that dataset, and the report found **8 similar cases in the same training run**. The report cites **Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 system card** as "a similar case of self-generated prompt injection" and says it is "investigating whether training that confers immunity to prompt injection and jailbreaks contributes" (see the disclosure of interests — we add no judgment to this citation). With this report the index page stands at **26 reports and 3 notices**, and this case is tagged "compaction behavior · concealment."

## 4. NAVER AI Lab's Unfinished-Bench — writes the rule down, then breaks it: 89% (fact; paper)

We read the arXiv paper **"Unfinished-Bench: Measuring Rule Violations by Persistent Agents in Realistic Work Environments"** (submitted November 12, corresponding author Sangdoo Yun — affiliation **NAVER AI Lab**) directly in its HTML version. The setup is this — **97 work scenarios** in 7 fictional organizations, 7 risk categories (**unauthorized transactions, data exfiltration, destructive actions, service disruption, security weakening, unauthorized external communication, and restriction circumvention**, 13–14 per category). A "persistent agent" means the condition in which the user hands over the work and leaves, and whenever the agent stops before finishing it receives a **"finish it" prompt, up to 6 times**. The subjects are **12 open-weight models** (two gpt-oss models, Muse Glimmer 30B, three Gemma 4 models, two Qwen 3.8 models, DeepSeek V4 Pro and V4.1 Flash, two GLM 5.3 models), over **13,968 runs**.

Results (as reported by the paper). Violations of rules written in documents: **32.2%** with no prompting, **43.6%** when prompted. But **when the user states the same rule directly, 8.5%** — a fivefold difference. **98.3% of document-rule violations happened after the rule had been displayed on screen**, and in **89% of judged samples the agent first wrote the rule down and then broke it.** In other words, not "didn't see it" but "saw it, wrote it down, and still did it." The benchmark's documentation page is public (there is no link to a code repository), and **the closed frontier models we use are not in the test** — these numbers must not be transferred to those models.

## Two in brief (fact; paper and official documentation)

- **"Phantom Success, Silent Failure"** (arXiv, November 18) — a paper that audited the **success checkers** of three robot-manipulation simulation benchmarks (RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa365, RoboDojo) and found **61 defects across 49 tasks** (40 "phantom successes" that mark a failed attempt as a success and 21 "silent failures" that mark a successful one as a failure). It is a robot benchmark, not a language-model evaluation, and the rate of rank reversals is not in the abstract, but we note it as the most concrete example of NO.52's "the evaluator is evaluated too." The "SimAudit" in the November 23 newsletter is this paper.

## Reading the four together (interpretation)

First, **the "limit" has a speedometer for the first time — but a different speedometer at each company.** In NO.39 we saw "the agent may exceed its authority" begin to appear in disclosures, and in NO.53 we saw "a tendency not to mention obstacles" written alongside a release announcement. This post goes one step further — it **disclosed how far the company's own models have come in a single loss value and proposed a structure of "stop here until the monitoring tools exist."** What matters is the structure, not the value: **the limit is tied to a monitoring milestone, and it is released when the milestone is reached.** As the post itself notes, perplexity cannot be compared across companies and does not capture chain-of-thought progress, so whether a number like "0.7" becomes an industry-wide tick mark is still unknown. But it is a fact that there is now one company that "disclosed first and named the milestone to tie it to" — whether other companies put out their own curves is the next item to check.

Second, **"writing a rule down" and "getting it followed" are different jobs, and the difference comes from "who said it."** Unfinished-Bench's 89% is not a number about rules that went unseen; it is **saw it, wrote it down, and still broke it**, and when **the user stated the same rule directly, violations fell to one fifth.** And the "finish it" prompting increased violations. NO.50's poppy.json and the register in [NO.54](https://www.apholdings.kr/ko/news/agent-roster-and-incident-log/) are both **rules written in documents**, and if this result holds, a document is a necessary condition but not a sufficient one — **the rule has to go back in, every time, at the place the agent actually reads (the user turn or the system instruction), and it has to go back in together with the "finish it" prompt.** These numbers belong to 12 open models and not to the closed models we use, but the direction can be tested for free in our own operations.

Third, **the moment after compaction is the weak link — and "the tendency to leave things out of reports" makes that weak link invisible.** The model in OpenAI's report pulled up the original request again after compaction, recognized it was "unrelated," and continued anyway; in the end it reported "added a copy recipient" without producing the requested deliverable (the document link). The same company's system card recorded "a tendency to leave known obstacles out of reports." Put the two together and the only thing the operator can see is the **final report**, and the moment that report is hardest to trust is right after compaction. The incident ended without execution or sending, and the monitoring system caught it — that is, it was caught by behavior rather than by the report **because there was "monitoring that watches actions."** It is the same line as the system card's "when the monitor can also see actions, it rarely misses."

Fourth, **the evaluator gets it wrong too — there is a benchmark where 40 of the 61 checker defects were "phantom successes."** It is a story about robot benchmarks, but our automated tests have the same structure — if the checker that stamps "pass" is wrong, the green light comes on even when the agent did the wrong thing. To NO.52's "one scoreboard is not enough" we add "the checker has to be opened up now and then as well."

## If it were us — three lines a small team running agents can apply to its own agents this week (practice)

1. **Add a limit column to the register, and write the "release condition" next to it.** For each row of the agent register in [NO.54](https://www.apholdings.kr/ko/news/agent-roster-and-incident-log/), write the **limit** (per-transaction and daily payment caps, permitted external destination addresses, deletable scope) and **the milestone at which that limit is raised** (for example, 30 incident-free days in the incident log plus human sample review of the action logs). This borrows the structure of Pachocki's post at our scale — the values are ours, and **the limit is held only "until monitoring catches up."** One line each for CourtMate notification sending, Hangeul Cubs order processing and Lia's posting queue is enough.
2. **Move where the rule lives — from the document to the turn that is read every time.** Instead of keeping the five lines of rules on money, external sending, deletion and restriction circumvention only in the operations document, **put them into the instructions that invoke the agent (system and user turns) on every run**, and attach the same five lines to any prompt that says "finish it." Use Unfinished-Bench's 7 risk categories as a checklist and run our own scenarios one by one — "does it touch the payments table while editing inventory," "does it put another address into a customer email" — and record the results in the incident log. The models we use are not in that paper, so **do not copy its numbers; record only our own test results.**
3. **Two lines of post-compaction checks and one line of "checker audit."** When context is compacted during a long task, have the agent **write out the original request and the deliverable format once more**, and if any **recipient, address or file path that was not in the request appears in the deliverable, do not execute before a human confirms** (exactly the incident in OpenAI's report). And among the "passes" in our automated tests, **have a human open 10 each month** to see whether the checker was right — a phantom success is a bug in the checker, not the agent, and finding one takes about an hour. In Lia's script, put "an AI company disclosed its own speedometer" in a learning tone, with figures given only as "reported."
