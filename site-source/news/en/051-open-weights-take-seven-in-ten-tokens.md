This week's three stories show where those numbers have flowed — **seven in ten tokens passing through the router go to open-weight models**, a new player holding the same price sheet has entered the smallest-model tier, and the tool that runs those models on your own computer reached 1.0. ## 1. Hugging Face → OpenRouter — Handing Over Routing and Publishing "Open Weights 71.7%" (facts, company-reported / platform measurement)

On November 16, Hugging Face posted **"Hugging Face and OpenRouter join forces to make open-weight models easy to run"** on its official blog. We read it directly. There are two parts. The first is a product change — in Hugging Face's "Inference Providers," **routing of language-model requests moves from its own router to OpenRouter's router.** Whichever provider serves an open-weight model on the Hub, you call it with a single Hugging Face token; for each request OpenRouter picks the provider by price, speed, and reliability, and if one fails it resends to another (previously, the post says, that provider's error was returned as-is). You can append `:fastest` or `:cheapest` or a provider name after the model name to choose, and if you send the same ID per conversation in a header (`X-HF-Session-Id`), it keeps you pinned to the provider that holds your prompt cache. It accepts OpenAI's Chat Completions and Responses formats and Anthropic's Messages format, and setup guides are attached for coding tools such as Claude Code, Codex, and OpenCode. **There is one breaking change — you can no longer use your own provider API keys for language models** (image and video keys are unchanged). Prices are the providers' prices as-is with no additional Hugging Face fee, the post says, and if you turn off a specific provider in settings the router will not send there. Image, video, and audio generation are not part of this change.

The second part is the numbers. All of them are **what OpenRouter counted from its own traffic.** The share of tokens going to open-weight models went from **26.8% in October 2025 → 71.7% at the end of September 2026** (peaking at 77.5% in July); over the same period, weekly open-weight token volume grew more than 60x while closed models grew about 10x. The most-used model this week was DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 at 59.2 trillion tokens. Average prices per 1M tokens were $0.28 input / $1.62 output for open weights and $1.27 / $6.36 for closed models — **about a 4x difference.** Citing the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, the post set the top open-weight model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, at 46 points and $3.68 per task next to the overall leader, Claude Fable 5.2, at 65 points and $26.54 (we opened the AA leaderboard ourselves on November 18 and confirmed the same numbers — a 19-point gap at about 7x the cost). OpenRouter's scale: **more than 800 trillion tokens a month, more than 10 million users, more than 600 models, more than 80 providers**, and after rebuilding the router, capacity errors down 46%, time to first token improved 16.9%, throughput up 16.3% (OpenRouter's own measurement). The post has no direct quotes and was published under the names of several Hugging Face staff.

We **opened the OpenRouter rankings (openrouter.ai/rankings) ourselves on November 18.** The top 10 by weekly tokens through November 16 were DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 (61.2T), Tencent Hy4 (16T), DeepSeek V4.1 Flash (15.5T), Z.ai GLM 5.3 Flash (11.8T), GPT-6 Luna (8.08T), Claude Opus 5.5 (7.37T), StepFun Step 5 Preview (6.36T), Xiaomi MiMo-V2.6-Flash (4.16T), GLM 5.3 (3.74T), and GPT-6.1 Sol (3.71T). **The top four are all models from Chinese companies**, and US models sit at 5th, 6th, and 10th. The slight difference in the number-one figure between the blog post (59.2T) and the rankings (61.2T) appears to be a difference in tally date, but we could not confirm this. The rankings text had no total-volume or open/closed-share numbers. For background, a Bloomberg chart relayed by OfficeChai (June 26) (data from OpenRouter and Exponential View) put the combined token share of Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic at about 70% in June 2025 → about 30% in June 2026 (approximate, no methodology).

One thing to note here. **Both companies had acquisition announcements this year.** Reuters (August 19) reported that Stripe had agreed to acquire OpenRouter (price undisclosed; a source said "a little over $8 billion"; Wikipedia relays Bloomberg's and the WSJ's "more than $7 billion"), and Stripe's CEO said "tokens are the central currency of companies building with AI." Nvidia announced on September 4 that it would acquire Hugging Face for $12.9 billion (Fortune, Advanced Television; reported as an "agreement," and we could not confirm whether it has closed), and Jensen Huang wrote on his blog that "Hugging Face will remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem, and using Nvidia compute is not required" (as quoted by Advanced Television). **The November 16 post mentions neither acquisition.** The OpenRouter scale figures in the Reuters article (10 trillion tokens a day, 10 million developers) and the company claim on Wikipedia (400 trillion tokens a month) are from different dates than the November post (800 trillion a month), so we left them as they are.

## 2. Meta Muse Ember — "Same Price, One-Third per Task" in the Smallest Tier (facts, company-reported / independent evaluation)

We read **"Introducing Our Smallest, Most Efficient Model: Muse Ember"** on the Meta AI research blog (November 9) directly. The intended use is "executing complex reasoning and the work agents repeat all day" — tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and parsing long documents. The price is **$0.10 input / $0.004 cached input / $0.50 output per 1M tokens**, and $0.30 / $0.012 / $1.50 for prompts over 200,000 tokens. Context is 1 million tokens, with a maximum output of 512,000 tokens. It is available through the Meta Model API (`muse-ember-1.0`), Muse Code, Google Cloud, and Oracle Cloud. The company's table compares it with GPT-6 Luna and Claude Haiku 5.5 at the maximum reasoning level — AutomationBench-AA 37.8% vs 20.2%/13.9%, Terminal-Bench 4.0 40.9% vs 16.4%/39.2%, OSWorld 2.1 69.5% vs 50.5%/68.9%, and **on FrontierCode 2.0-AA it is 27.2% vs 25.2%/34.9%, where Haiku leads.** An Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index of 36 is "tied with Haiku 5.5, above Luna (28)," and **"matched Haiku 5.5's top score at about one-third the cost"** is the company's summary sentence. Against its own higher-tier model Muse Spark 1.3 (index 37, about $2.39 per task), it is 1 point lower at about one-seventeenth the cost. The post itself notes that several benchmarks are internal measurements and that some use a specific harness or partial scoring. Before release, it says, the model was evaluated under the "Meta Superintelligence Scaling Framework" for chemical, biological, cyber, and loss-of-control risks and came in below the high-risk threshold, and went through agentic-behavior evaluations (refusing instructions hidden in web pages and tool outputs, asking a human before high-impact actions). **There is no mention of open weights.**

We opened the independent evaluation side directly. **Artificial Analysis's Muse Ember page** lists an Intelligence Index (v5.0.1) of 36 at the max level (2nd of 36 in its class) and 34 at xhigh, **$0.14 per task**, output speed of 449 tokens/second, and 3.1 minutes per task (the class median is intelligence 24 and prices of $0.24/$0.83). The **AA overall leaderboard** (November 18) shows Claude Haiku 5.5 Max at 36 points and $0.41 per task, GPT-6 Luna Max at 28 points and $0.15, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017 Max at 33 points and $0.60, and Muse Ember Max at 36 points and $0.14 — Meta's "one-third" matches the $0.14 vs $0.41 in this table. What Simon Willison (November 10) pointed out is the price sheet — **Ember, Luna, and Haiku 5.5 all have the same short-prompt price of $0.10/$0.50**, and what differs is the "long prompt" threshold (200,000 / 272,000 / 100,000 tokens) and the price beyond it (Haiku is $0.50/$2.50). So he wrote that "price per token is a weak comparison — the number of tokens spent on the same task differs by model," and charted AA's cost per task (Ember xhigh about $0.12, max $0.14; Haiku xhigh about $0.25; Luna xhigh about $0.10). In his own tests, he wrote, "high" among the six reasoning levels failed to finish an SVG, and the level did not cleanly predict output quality. **Whether Ember's weights are released appears nowhere — not in Meta's post, the AA page, or Willison's post.**

## 3. llama.cpp 1.0.0 — The Local Runtime Promised "If It Breaks, the Number Goes Up" (facts, project announcement)

We read **"Hot llama.cpp v1.0.0: A stronger foundation for the local AI ecosystem"** directly, posted by ggml-org on the Hugging Face blog (November 13). llama.cpp is a C/C++ runtime for running LLMs on your own computer (laptop, server, mobile device), and it put 1.0.0 on two libraries (`libllama` and `libggml`) **about three and a half years** after the first commit. The promise is **semantic versioning** — a change that breaks the public C API bumps the major number, compatible features and new model support bump the minor, bug fixes bump the patch. The post lists more than 150 supported model architectures, support for the mainstream speculative decoding methods, distribution via Docker, Debian, Homebrew, conda-forge, MacPorts, Winget, and XCFramework, and CI running on Hugging Face Buckets and Jobs plus self-hosted runners provided by **Nvidia, AMD, Qualcomm, Arm, IBM, and Intel.** The post has no GitHub star count, download count, contributor count, or license (we did not open the repository), and the comments include a note that processing speed on very long prompts is still a challenge and a question asking "is there no Linux desktop app?" (the desktop apps the post lists are macOS and Windows).

## Reading the Three Together (interpretation)

First, **tokens flow along the price sheet, not the scoreboard — but we have to write down "tokens passing through which door."** The 71.7% is the share of tokens passing through one router, OpenRouter. Tokens from companies contracting directly with model makers, and tokens running inside clouds, are not in this number. In NO.16 we wrote "which AI is used most — why the numbers disagree"; Ramp's corporate-card payment data (NO.47: by US corporate payments, Anthropic 44.8%, OpenAI 40.6%) and OpenRouter's token data (top four are Chinese models) are the same market in the same month seen from different doors. Both can be right — one is "who pays," the other is "which model eats the tokens." And token volume is not money (NO.43's "token volume up 37%, spending up 9%"). Taking the average prices from the Hugging Face post as-is (open $0.28/$1.62, closed $1.27/$6.36) and doing a very rough calculation, even with 71.7% of tokens going to open weights, **in money terms it most likely does not exceed half** (our calculation; since we do not know the input/output ratio, this is only order-of-magnitude — do not carry the number itself anywhere). In other words, "seven-tenths" is seven-tenths of volume, not of revenue.

Second, **the smallest tier has become one where the price sheet is identical, so you cannot choose by price sheet.** Ember, Luna, and Haiku 5.5 all have a short-prompt price of $0.10/$0.50. What differs is cost per task ($0.14, $0.15, $0.41, per AA), the long-prompt threshold (200,000, 272,000, 100,000 tokens), and the score (36, 28, 36). In NO.46 we wrote "price per token ≠ price per task" using Hydrogen and Flash; this time, **three models with the same price split 3x on cost per task** — because the number of tokens they spend on the same job differs, and that number comes only from independent evaluations and our own records, not from the manufacturer. That Meta's "one-third" matches the AA table is a good sign, but AA's cost per task is the value on AA's own evaluation set, which does not mean the same ratio will appear in our work (NO.21). We also note that the company's table left in the losing cell, "Haiku leads on FrontierCode" — as with Anthropic's table in NO.44, a table with a losing cell is more worth reading than one without (interpretation).

Third, **the owners of the platforms producing the numbers have changed.** OpenRouter, which counted the 71.7% open-weight figure, and Hugging Face, which published it, are companies that announced agreements to be acquired by a payments company and a chip company, respectively. There is no evidence that either company distorted that number, and we do not write that — but the explanation "a clean signal because it is a neutral marketplace" (the phrasing in OfficeChai's June article) now needs one more line: "a marketplace owned by a payments company." Huang's "will remain an open platform; Nvidia compute not required" is an answer to that question, and the Hugging Face post's breaking change ("you can no longer use your own provider keys") and the "you can turn providers off" toggle are a small version of the same question — **when the router chooses it is convenient, but in exchange I cannot see who is choosing.** On the opposite side stands llama.cpp 1.0. In NO.48 we wrote "only what you keep outside the model carries over to the next model"; the runtime that runs the model is also "something kept outside," and 1.0 is a promise that the API of that outside box will not break, at least within a major version. The three stories come down to the same three options — leave it to a router, call the manufacturer's API directly, or run it on your own computer — and the difference between the three is not price but **"who chooses, where the data goes, and who fixes it when it breaks."**

## What We Would Do — Three Things a Team Running Commerce, Apps, and an AI Influencer Should Do This Week (practice)

1. **Add a "route" column to the model cost table.** Next to the "cost per task" column we built in NO.46, write down which door each model is called through — manufacturer API direct / router (OpenRouter, Hugging Face Inference Providers, etc.) / local (llama.cpp) — and attach three cells to each route: who picks the provider (us or the router), which company's servers the prompt and response pass through (for routers, record the provider list), and whether we can use our own key (Hugging Face: not for language models as of November 16). For any job that uses a router, actually save the **"providers turned off" list** in settings and record the date. For requests containing customer data, such as ingredient-label judgments (NO.03), make it a one-line rule: do not send them through "a route where we do not choose the provider."
2. **Measure the three smallest-tier models on the same 20 cases.** From the high-repetition jobs — short corrections to Lia's (our AI influencer's) scripts, classifying commerce inquiries, extracting ingredient-label items — take 20 cases and run them on Muse Ember, Claude Haiku 5.5, and GPT-6 Luna at the **same reasoning level**, recording completed cases, actual input/output token counts, cost per task, and time taken (the same table as the four cells of NO.48's "model-swap rehearsal"). If you write the three models' long-prompt thresholds (200,000 / 100,000 / 272,000) in the table header, you can see at a glance which threshold our prompts cross. AA's $0.14/$0.41/$0.15 goes in a "reference value" column — and once our numbers come in, they sit next to it.
3. **Pick one local 1.0 test.** Spin up one small open-weight model with llama.cpp 1.0.0 on an office computer, and see whether 20 ingredient-label judgment cases involving **questions mixed with personal information (allergies, medications being taken)** can be processed without leaving the building — recording judgment quality, response time, and the hardware specs we can afford. Add a **runtime name and version cell** (llama.cpp 1.0.x) to NO.48's "box outside the model." If the results are bad, write that they are bad — the purpose of this test is not to move to local, but to have the boundary between "work that can move and work that cannot" in numbers.
