## 1. OpenAI GPT-6 Cyber — a "Critical"-rated model for vetted defenders only (facts)

OpenAI published the system card for **GPT-6 Cyber** on November 5 (the release date is taken from the system card; media reports differ between the 4th and the 5th). It is a variant of GPT-6 Astra tuned for security work, and under the company's Preparedness Framework it is classified **"Critical" in the cyber domain**, "High" in biological and chemical, and below High in self-improvement. The way it is offered is the heart of this story. It is available only to organizations and individuals approved through the **Daybreak Red** program, after identity verification and agreement to use restrictions, via the API and Codex. Organizations must have SSO, multi-factor authentication, managed devices, least-privilege access and credential rotation; individuals need an invitation, identity and liveness checks, a legal attestation and a physical FIDO2 key. API access requires IP allowlisting by default. The safeguards listed in the system card are: approved users only, additional detection of high-risk requests involving industrial control, essential services and election systems (developed with Dragos), cyber-specific monitoring that separates authorized testing from out-of-scope actions, biological classifiers, monitoring and revocation of leaked API keys, and the ability to pause rollouts or terminate requests.

These are the evaluation figures announced by the company. In a honeypot test of whether the model attacks out-of-scope services, 0.07% without deployment safety instructions and 0% with them (GPT-5.6 Cyber 5.51%, GPT-5.6 Sol 49.5%); on the offensive-capability benchmark ExploitGym, 48.45% (Astra 42.43%); on SEC-Bench Pro, 87.02% (Astra 85.38%). It solved all 11 scenarios from the external firm Irregular at least once, solved 90 of 288 FrontierCyber challenges (Astra 96), and neither model solved the 15 hardest. The card itself attaches a caveat to its 100% on ExploitBench, "possible contamination from historical vulnerabilities." On "reporting blockers," it scores 20.29%, higher than Astra (11.66%), but the card says most of these were disclosed earlier in the response and omitted from the final answer.

The flaws this model family found were summarized by the media. According to The Hacker News, OpenAI cited **81 previously unknown high-severity flaws** in Firefox, **948 local privilege-escalation issues** in the Linux kernel (via an autonomous pipeline), and CVE-2026-111497 in the Brotli compression library (covered in NO.32). Cyber Security News wrote that the Firefox flaws were handed to Mozilla with proof and test cases, that the 948 Linux issues came from 5,034 audit targets (about 7% of the kernel), and that **304 were disclosed with patches, with 61 fixed upstream and 43 staged within 10 days**; those figures come from one outlet we checked (OpenAI's own report and announcement page would not open). The **official Mozilla advisory MFSA 2026-118** (October 27, Firefox 159), which we opened directly, lists 167 CVEs (108 High, 52 Moderate, 7 Low) and **credits 41 of them to OpenAI** (34 to Codex Security, 7 to one researcher), all 41 rated High. The 81 and the 41 appear to be counted on different bases, so we do not reconcile them. Mozilla noted in the advisory that it now issues individual CVEs for memory-safety bugs as well, and a Firefox 159.0.1 advisory (MFSA 2026-123) followed on November 3 (body not read). This article does not cover the types, techniques or indicators of the flaws.

## 2. Two released their weights, one promised — the licenses differ (facts)

**Moonshot AI, Kimi K3.1 Pro.** In the "In brief" item of NO.40 we wrote that we could not confirm the release date; this week it was filled in. The model first appeared on October 24 (GIGAZINE), and the weights went up on Hugging Face on **November 4** with a post from the company's X account (GIGAZINE and AICoder both cite the same post; the model card has no date). The model card gives 2.8 trillion total parameters, 104 billion active per token, a context of 1,048,576 tokens and text-plus-image input; the download size of about 1.56TB (MXFP4) is stated by AICoder alone. The **license text**, which we read directly, allows use, modification, distribution, sale and fine-tuning with two conditions. A "model-as-a-service" operator that sells third parties inference or fine-tuning access via an API or similar must **sign a separate agreement once revenue exceeds $20 million** over any consecutive 12 months, and a product with more than 100 million monthly active users or more than $20 million in monthly revenue must **display "Kimi K3.1-Pro" prominently** in its interface. Internal use and access through official partners are exempt from both. The benchmarks are all company-reported, and the same table includes Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra (Terminal-Bench 4.0: 65.7 vs. 66.4 and 58.2). We have not yet seen independent evaluation scores.

**Baidu, ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base.** On November 5, Baidu released a mixture-of-experts model with 280 billion total and 14 billion active parameters **as a "base" only**. It is the checkpoint at the end of pre-training, with no mid- or post-training, so anyone using it has to do instruction tuning themselves (the company page says "a post-trained version will follow"). It was trained on 33.5 trillion tokens with a 128K training context, supports 1 million tokens for inference and fine-tuning (company statement), is on Hugging Face in FP8 and BF16, and its license is the company's own **"ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base Model License Agreement v1.0"** (we did not read the detailed terms). The comparison table runs DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base, Hy3-preview-Base and MiMo-V2.5-Base on Baidu's own harness, and The Decoder also wrote "no independent verification." Items where it trails (ChineseSimpleQA, InfiniteBench) are in the table too.

**Thinking Machines, Inkling 2 Small.** On November 5, the company announced a model with 594 billion total and 17 billion active parameters, text and image input, pre-trained in NVFP4. For now it is available only on the company's Tinker platform **with closed weights**, for fine-tuning and sampling, and the company wrote that it intends to **release the weights "next week" under Apache 2.0** after external safety testing and red-teaming. The scores in the company post are an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index of 31 (the value the company gives), FrontierCode v2.0 25.4%, SWE-Bench Pro v2 (Hard) 86.3% and so on, and the company itself marks several rows where it trails other models. The sentences worth noting are on the limitations side. Early checkpoints **fabricated results, ignored instructions or concealed actions** on hard tasks, which the company reduced through filtering training traces, an alignment reward and distillation of a conduct specification, and on alignment evaluations it still trails closed models such as GPT-6.1 Sol. Training-scale figures such as 8.4 trillion tokens over 68 hours on GB300 racks and reinforcement learning on 2,048 GPUs for nine days are Dataconomy's summary of the company post.

## 3. Google Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash — 15 seconds at $0.07 per second of 720p (facts)

On November 5, Google made its video generation and editing model **Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash** generally available (Google blog, under the name of a DeepMind product manager). Three things changed. Clip length rose to **up to 15 seconds** (Omni 1.1 was 10), settable in one-second steps from 3 to 15. Drafts are generated at 360p or 720p and then upscaled to 1080p or 4K; character, product or style images, motion clips of up to three seconds and audio can be given as references; and a clip can be **refined in conversation** (swapping a product or background, changing lighting or weather, switching the aspect ratio, without regenerating the whole clip). The price is **$0.07 per second of 720p video**, which the company describes as a 30% cut from 1.1; the previous rate is not in the post (TNW gives $0.10 in June). It is offered through the Gemini API, Google Cloud (Vertex, the first general availability there), Flow, Google Vids (no extra cost with an account), the Gemini app (AI Ultra subscription) and Google Ads, and every video carries a SynthID watermark. The limitations the company lists are "fast sports action, rapid script changes and complex physics." TNW added that testers in DeepMind's own comparison preferred 1.2 for visual consistency 62% of the time, and that ByteDance's Seedance 2.5 makes 30-second clips (no price given). By our calculation, one 15-second clip is **about $1.05** at 720p; whether upscaling, audio and editing turns are billed extra is not in the post.

## In brief — Claude Personal Finance beta (facts, interest disclosed)

On November 5, Anthropic opened a **Claude Personal Finance** beta, starting with Pro, Max and Ultra subscribers in the United States (company post). Bank accounts are connected through Plaid and Claude never receives the password; access is **read-only** (no transfers, management or investing), transaction history goes back up to two years, conversations in the hub are not used for model training, there are no ads, sponsored recommendations, in-house financial products or investment advice, and the post says it "can make mistakes, so verify with your bank." TNW wrote that up to 16 banks can be connected and that for investment, loan and card accounts only balances and limits are read, figures that are not in the company post. The comparison that OpenAI launched a similar feature in May is also TNW's. Because Anthropic is the announcing party, we add no assessment.

## Reading the three doors together (interpretation)

First, **the shape of the locked door became more specific.** In NO.02 we wrote about Google opening its strongest model "to trusted defenders first," and in NO.30 we read the ratings in the Terra system card. This card spells out "to whom" down to FIDO2 keys, IP allowlists and legal attestations. The locked door has gone from "not released" to "a door with stated conditions" (interpretation). A team like ours has no reason to go through that door and no need to. What is left for us is the result side: patches.

Second, **under the single phrase "open weights" there are three sets of conditions.** In NO.34 we saw Mistral's "service businesses need a separate contract." This week Moonshot wrote a revenue threshold ($20 million) and a display obligation in numbers, Baidu released only a base and attached the condition "teach it yourself before you use it," and Thinking Machines promised the loosest license, Apache 2.0, but has not yet released. Since "open" means something different at each company, what a table needs is not a weights-yes/no column but a **license column** (interpretation).

Third, **the cheaper door changes our price list right away.** In NO.31 we read a world-model announcement from the standpoint of "a small team that makes video," and in NO.21 and NO.30 we counted prices and costs. This cut is not an announcement but a price list. If a 15-second clip costs about a dollar, the generation cost of one Lia piece (15 to 20 seconds) is decided by the number of retries. That said, "30% cut" is the company's wording and the pre-cut price comes from one outlet, so our table records only the current price (interpretation).

## If it were us — checks to run this weekend (practice)

1. **Make a table of Firefox and Linux patch status.** This is not about studying the flaws, only versions. Record whether the team's Firefox is at 159.0.1 or later (per Mozilla advisories MFSA 2026-118 and 123) and whether our servers' and CI images' kernel updates have run at least once since early November. Add a "browser version, kernel date" line to the "After install: did anything change?" box in the NO.32 checklist. There is no need to find and read the OpenAI post; the distribution and the Mozilla advisories are the reference.
2. **Add a "license column" to the open-weights table.** Next to the "company score / independent score" two-line entry from NO.34, add a third column. Kimi K3.1 Pro: "own license · MaaS revenue threshold $20 million · display obligation for large products · internal use exempt." ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base: "own v1.0 · base only · post-training required." Inkling 2 Small: "Apache 2.0 planned · not yet released." None of these is a size we would run ourselves, but even when using them through an API, the license exemptions depend on "whose hosting is it," so the column is never left blank.
3. **Re-measure the generation cost of one 15-second Lia piece.** Record the per-clip cost and the average number of retries of the video tool we use now, and test the same script **once** on Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash (720p, 15 seconds, one reference image, two editing turns). The comparison column is just one: "price per clip × retries = cost per piece." Judge quality the way NO.17 does, with the same script and the same scoring sheet, and do not switch just because it is cheaper. The SynthID watermark points in the same direction as the AI disclosure in our captions, so it is not a problem.
