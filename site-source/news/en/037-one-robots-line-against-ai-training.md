For three years, teams running a website have had to look up, company by company, "where do I write what, to stop our articles being used for AI training." Google had Google-Extended, OpenAI had GPTBot, Anthropic had ClaudeBot, Apple had Applebot-Extended. Different names, different scopes. In NO.28 we covered a survey finding AI summaries on 88% of commercial searches; in NO.25, what web share figures fail to count. This week's news is the other side of that: **for the first time, the way a site says "here is how AI may use my content" has been gathered into one line.** But a line existing and a line being honored are different things, so we separate the facts from the limits.

## 1. Four companies agreed on one token — the IETF draft "__usage_ai-training" (facts)

On October 30, Kevin Kelley, Anthropic's technical lead for standards, submitted a draft to the IETF titled **"Machine-Readable Training Preference Signal."** Its document number is draft-kelley-aipref-training-preference-signal-00, and it is an **individual draft** that expires on May 3, 2027. Its header says it is "not endorsed by the IETF" and has "no formal standing." The draft defines one thing: a **policy token, `__usage_ai-training`**, written where a User-Agent group name goes in robots.txt. A Disallow under that group expresses a preference that "content at this path should not be used for AI training"; an Allow expresses a preference that it may be. "Training" follows the vocabulary draft of the IETF AI Preferences working group: **"using an asset to modify the learned parameters of a generative AI model."**

The same day, four companies said they would honor the token. We confirmed three of them directly. **Microsoft** wrote on the Bing blog that it is "joining industry peers in announcing a shared AI training preference in robots.txt," with enforcement from **February 1, 2027**. **Google** wrote on its Search Central blog that it "plans to voluntarily adopt" a proposal under IETF consideration, which "will help websites express their AI training preferences across multiple AI providers in a single step" (our fetch of the post showed only navigation, so we confirmed the wording through Search Engine Roundtable and PPC Land), and added a note to the Google-Extended entry in its crawler documentation that support is intended for **"early 2027."** **Anthropic**'s draft author told the IETF mailing list that the company "has already started implementing support," without saying which crawlers or when, and with caveats: "it'll take us some time," "this complements our existing crawler controls rather than replacing them," and "how we interpret and apply these signals may change." **OpenAI**'s official post was blocked for our fetch; Engadget reported OpenAI pledged to implement it "in the coming months," while PPC Land wrote that no timeline was given.

The draft has a few more rules an operator should know. First, **the token is not a crawling directive.** It says content-usage preferences and crawler traffic rules are "independent." Second, **the `User-Agent: *` wildcard does not apply to this token.** Only a group that names the token is read as a preference. Third, specific expressions take precedence over general ones, and "private agreements MAY override preferences expressed via this mechanism." Fourth, preferences apply based on the robots.txt "in effect when the asset was retrieved." Engadget put that as: the directive "cannot undo any past scraping," and it "doesn't prevent crawling altogether or prevent content from showing up on Google search."

Google's interpretation was explained by Google Search Advocate John Mueller (Search Engine Roundtable, PPC Land). If a site blocks **only the shared token** without mentioning Google-Extended, Google will "interpret that as if the publisher had disallowed Google-Extended." Google's crawler documentation says Google-Extended is not a crawler of its own but a token used "in a control capacity," governing both **training of Gemini models** and **grounding in Gemini Apps and Vertex AI** (providing content from the Search index to the model at prompt time), and that it **does not affect inclusion in Google Search or ranking**. Pushback from the Google side appeared in the same coverage. Google's Gary Illyes said robots.txt is a suggestion, not a security control — "anyone can impersonate Googlebot in about 30 seconds" (a podcast remark relayed by PPC Land) — and IETF participant Nate Hake said "it's not actually done at the ietf level" with "significant areas of dispute," and that the draft "basically exempts the preferences from applying to anything a user does" (Search Engine Roundtable).

## 2. Bing split "training" from "citing in answers" — three settings and four dates (facts)

On the same October 30, Microsoft Bing announced its own controls on top of the shared token (Krishna Madhavan, Bing blog). The core is **separating two uses.** In the company document's definitions, **"training" is using content to modify the learned parameters of a generative AI model**, and **"grounding" is using content as input to help inform AI-generated responses.** The document says traditional indexing and ranking are not grounding.

The settings work like this. **Training** has two values, Allow and Disallow: site-wide through the `__usage_ai-training` group in robots.txt, page-level through `<meta name="Bingbot" content="ai-training:disallow">` or an equivalent X-Robots-Tag header. Page-level overrides robots.txt. **Grounding** has three values, Allow / DisallowExtended / Disallow, and is set **at page level only** (meta or header). There is no site-wide robots.txt setting for it. DisallowExtended permits citation only in experiences Microsoft operates itself (AI Mode, AI Overview, Copilot, Microsoft 365) and refuses it in grounding services sold to third parties. **If nothing is written, both default to Allow.** The company document says these preferences "do not change eligibility for traditional Bing search results," while also saying that setting grounding to Disallow or DisallowExtended will "significantly reduce" visibility in AI-generated experiences. It adds that neither choice guarantees inclusion, traffic or payment.

There are four dates. **December 15, 2026:** temporary grounding settings open in Bing Webmaster Tools (only for sites using NoArchive or NoCache as of that day). **February 1, 2027:** the new preferences take effect as pages are crawled. After **March 1, 2027,** NoArchive and NoCache, used since 2023, are no longer read as training or grounding preferences. **September 30, 2027:** the temporary grounding settings expire. The document adds that IndexNow can request a recrawl but does not guarantee a date.

## Reading the two together (interpretation)

First, **one line is a declaration, not a lock.** The draft itself says it is "not a directive for crawling behavior," and a Google employee says impersonation takes thirty seconds. What this line does is make four companies read "please don't train on our content" the same way. Whether they honor it depends on each company's promise, and a site currently has no way to verify that they did. Still, the past three years' chore of hunting each company's name shrinks. For a small team in particular, the real change is that **"edit robots.txt every time a new AI company appears" becomes "check whether that company accepts one token"** (interpretation).

Second, **training and citing are different questions.** Only Bing split the two; the shared token covers training only. Google reads the token as a Google-Extended block, and by Google's documentation Google-Extended governs not just training but also grounding in Gemini Apps and Vertex. So how a site tells Google "refuse training, allow citation in Gemini answers" with one shared token is a gap. We could not find this in Google's documentation and leave it as a question to be answered when Google starts support in 2027. Bing's line that blocking citation "significantly reduces" visibility in AI experiences is an honest one, and points the same way as the "AI summaries on 88% of commercial searches" we saw in NO.28. **Refusing training and refusing citation carry different costs for a site** (interpretation).

Third, **"what a user asks for" sits outside.** As Hake pointed out, the draft effectively exempts fetches a user directly requests, and Kelley's mail says interpretation "may change." Our articles being read when an AI chat answers someone's question continues regardless of this token. Whether to block that is a separate decision, and we are on the side of not blocking. AP News is written to be read (interpretation).

## If it were us — three things to decide this week (practice)

1. **Sort content into three groups and fill two columns, "training / citing."** When we opened our site's robots.txt, it had a single `User-agent: *` group with Allow plus folder Disallows (source, scripts, backups, docs, and the four languages awaiting review) — and not one AI token. Under the draft's rules `*` does not apply to the token, so **we currently express no preference at all**, and by Bing's defaults both are Allow. So first we build a decision table. ①AP News, product pages and the company page: "training allowed, citing allowed" — they are written to be read. ②Source, script, backup and internal-document folders are already blocked from crawling, but that is a traffic rule, not a training preference, so we write the same paths as Disallow under the token group too. ③Pages where user content mixes in (reviews, boards, inquiries): "training refused," and citing set to DisallowExtended on Bing. The table sits next to the permissions table from NO.29 and is reviewed quarterly.
2. **When writing it, add the token group separately and keep the company-specific names.** The draft, Google and Anthropic all say existing controls keep working, so we **add** the `__usage_ai-training` group and do not delete existing groups such as Google-Extended, GPTBot and ClaudeBot. We do not expect anything written under `*` to apply to the token. Bing's grounding preference is not in robots.txt and works only as a page-level meta or header, so it goes only into the template for group ③ pages. On the calendar: **February 1, 2027** (Bing enforcement) and **March 1, 2027** (NoArchive/NoCache no longer read as preferences), and in the first week of February we reopen each company's documentation to see whether Google and OpenAI have actually started support. If an official document will not open, we cross-check with two outlets.
3. **Keep "written" and "honored" in different columns.** A site has no way to know whether this token was honored. So we do only what we can. **Once a month** we count visits by AI crawler names (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Bingbot and so on) in server logs, and note in the table that Google-Extended has no crawler name of its own and will not appear in logs. The report that click data may not come to Google Search Console's AI performance report (an Illyes remark, Search Engine Roundtable's November 2 recap, one source) is kept as a flagged reference only. In three months we check each company's documentation again for changes to the definition of "training." We do not report "we wrote the preference" as "AI doesn't use our content."
