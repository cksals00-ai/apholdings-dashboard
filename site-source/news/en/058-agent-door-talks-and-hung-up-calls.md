We ourselves are on the store side — we run commerce (Hangul Cups), a community app (CourtMate) and an AI influencer (Lia), so we are a party to the question "how does a store receive an agent." In NO.43 we wrote "Amazon blocked Meta's Muse"; in NO.45, 48 and 53, the shape of United, eBay and Signal closing their doors; and in NO.55, "there are four doors for a store to decide on." This week, two opposite directions showed up in front of that door at the same time — the largest store that had shut its door sat down at the negotiating table, and in Korea the three telcos and Naver for the first time hung up on "the agent's call." And on the cost side, "pay only for what you used" came down to the file level. We lay out media reports (Fortune, TNW, The Paypers, The Decoder, TechCrunch, VentureBeat, ZDNet Korea, The Public, Youth Daily, Money Today), company announcements (two from Google, one from Anthropic), a specification document (the MCP skills extension) and a survey report (LeadDev) in three parts: facts, interpretation, and what we would do.

## 1. Amazon–Meta — negotiating "search results and ads" over the door closed in September; no deal yet (facts, media reports)

We read Fortune (Sebastian Herrera, November 25, "Exclusive") directly. Amazon and Meta are **discussing a potential deal and have met several times** over access to Amazon for Meta's personal agent Muse (NO.21, 22, 43, 53); the sources are people familiar with the matter, and the piece says **whether a deal will happen, and if so when, is unknown**. Two issues were cited — **how search results are handled** when Muse shows Amazon products, and **how ads are handled**. Amazon's concerns, as written, are lost ad revenue, the quality of the search results Muse would show, and the possibility that shopping agents put distance between consumers and Amazon; a deal is expected to need the approval of both companies' CEOs. The block came in September (Amazon said Muse's access violated its terms of service), the same month Meta launched Muse in the US. The figures are Fortune's own description — Muse had **more than 10 million people set it up** in the US and Canada as of October (link to its own October 22 article), and Amazon's ad business is **a $68.6 billion-a-year business, close to 10% of revenue** (no source given). It also relayed two remarks by Andy Jassy — in October, that outside shopping agents are "not yet good and not more convenient than coming to the store directly," and at a Semafor event this month, that "if there is a fair value exchange, we don't rule out outside agents." Jassy also said more than 400 million people used Amazon's own "Alexa for shopping" over the past year (company remark). Meta brought in Amazon competitors such as Shopify, Walmart and Best Buy as partners in Muse's early days (Fortune; whether they are actually live is not stated), and Zuckerberg has said he plans to take a "small fee" on transactions. **Both Meta and Amazon declined to comment.** The citation of a 2025 Morgan Stanley report ($190 billion to $385 billion of US e-commerce via agent shopping in 2030) is a forecast, so we only relay it.

The Next Web (Cristian Dina, November 25) relayed Fortune and added numbers from CNBC (Julia Boorstin, November 24) — **by Forter Intelligence's tally, of the orders placed by agents in the week through November 22, 51% were Muse, Grok 24%, ChatGPT 22%**, with the rest Google and Instinct. The sentence that Zuckerberg said on the earnings call that "25% of Muse users have shopped with the agent, with an average purchase of more than $200," and a senior Meta executive's "it lets advertisers get more return from the money they already spend" (in the context of Muse helping Instagram ads), are also CNBC citations. We did not open the CNBC original, so we treat these numbers as **one chain: CNBC as relayed by TNW**. The Paypers (November 26) noted that Muse users saw error messages at Amazon checkout in September and that after Amazon's block eBay also restricted outside agents (the terms we read directly in NO.48), and The Decoder (November 25), relaying Fortune and CNBC, carried both Jassy's "third-party agent shopping is not yet more convenient" and "did not rule out the possibility of opening the door."

In NO.43 we wrote "customers cannot bring their own agent." Two months later the picture is a little different — the largest store has put "how search results and ads are handled," rather than "absolutely not," on the table. That does not mean the door has opened. It means the names of the conditions have been made public.

## 2. SKT, KT, LG Uplus and Naver — they decided not to take "the agent's call," and behind it is one app released in October (facts, media reports)

We read ZDNet Korea (Hong Ji-hoo and Park Seo-rin, November 24, morning) directly. The three telcos and Naver **will not provide customer-service consultations to outside AI agents that call the customer center on a user's behalf**. The article gives no start date and does not say how they technically tell the agents apart. Three reasons were cited — the agents' reliability is unverified, personal information could be exposed or misused, and even with delegation it is hard to confirm that the caller is the actual user. Naver's explanation (anonymous) was that "it is unclear whether an agent is legally the user's representative, and we cannot tell whether the requester is the user or someone else," and the article says **Naver does not proceed with a consultation if the caller identifies itself as an "AI agent"** (this sentence is in ZDNet only). The customer centers' own AI is used only for simple inquiries such as payment methods and website use, while matters requiring identity verification, such as device installment details, are handled by people.

The Public (Yang Won-mo, November 24, afternoon) described each company's response in more detail (each in this article only). SKT, when an AI assistant calls, confirms the subscriber's contact details, ends the call, and has a representative call the subscriber back; KT does not consult with outside AI because identity verification is difficult; LG Uplus set a principle of no phone consultations with AI assistants; Naver does not respond to consultation requests from outside AI. The core logic is that "a plan change requires identity verification, and **consenting to an AI proxy call in an app is not the formal representative verification the carrier requires**," and a telecom industry source (anonymous) said strict identity verification will be maintained for the time being because a malfunction or security incident could lead to disputes with subscribers.

The app behind this news is **Pickle**. According to another ZDNet Korea article the same day (Park Seo-rin, November 24, afternoon), it is an agent app from a Silicon Valley startup founded by Koreans that began its Korean beta in October, and it makes restaurant and hospital reservations, calls customer centers, and shops and pays on the user's behalf (the integration list includes Google Calendar, Government24, Coupang, Musinsa, Kurly, Yanolja and Hyundai Card). The Mobile Index (IGAWorks) figures the article cited — weekly new installs of 67,583 for November 2–8 → 129,394 for 9–15 → 139,504 for 16–22, and weekly active users of 37,886 for October 26–November 1 → 286,996 for "the same period" (the comparison window is ambiguously labeled). **These numbers are a data company citation in one article.** The same article carries Kakao's position — Pickle opened a KakaoTalk integration on November 11 and suspended it within a day (Pickle explained it as an integration speed problem), and Kakao, while saying "we have taken no measures to block Pickle," explained that this integration worked by having users hand their PC KakaoTalk ID and password to Pickle so the agent could operate KakaoTalk, and said it is reviewing the policy and legal issues. The article also has cases of Pickle sending emails the user had not confirmed or changing a schedule without asking, and Pickle's side said it operates within the account permissions the user has granted and is discussing official integrations and partnerships. It also says Naver is preparing a personal agent app within the year and Kakao a "Kanana Space" pilot next month (each in one outlet).

Two days later, two outlets framed this trend as "spreading." Youth Daily (Ahn Jeong-hoon, November 26) cited overseas cases — JPMorgan, Citi and Bank of America blocking outside AI from account access (as relayed from American Banker), United Airlines (the original we read directly in NO.45), and the Japanese restaurant-reservation agent "AutoReserve" repeatedly calling for tens of minutes even after being asked to stop, leading some restaurants to block the number and announce "we do not take reservations through this service" — and as alternatives listed "Know Your Agent," under discussion in the US (verify an agent's identity and scope of authority first, then allow limited access), separating public information (hours, prices) from sensitive actions (account lookups, contract changes) into tiers, and logging agent behavior. Money Today (November 26, republished on Supple) added to that — the case relayed by American Banker of "an outside AI at one large bank resetting a password, changing the accounts approved for transfers, and withdrawing $100,000," Reuters (November 23) on "US banks considering kill switches even for their own agents," Jamie Dimon's remark on the Q3 earnings call ("we will not let any outside agent into our systems"), and **the statement by the head of the AI policy office at the Ministry of Science and ICT at a November 25 meeting that a legal definition and rules for AI agents are under review** (Money Today only). Hanyang University professor Yoon Hye-sun's remark that "no country has settled on an answer yet, and amending the AI Basic Act alone will likely not be enough" is in the same article. We did not open the originals for the $100,000 case or the Reuters report.

## 3. "Pay only for what you used" has moved inside the file, and "limits" are being merged or left to be broken (facts, company announcements and media)

**Google Gemini API "file slicing controls" (November 23)** — we read the official post by Google DeepMind's Rohan Doshi and Ben Kane directly. Instead of feeding in a whole file, **only the needed pages, cells, segments or layers are cut server-side and fed to the model, and tokens are billed "only for the part the model inspected."** The newly supported native upload formats, beyond PDF, are Microsoft Office (docx, slides, xlsx and so on) and OpenDocument, and audio and video are also covered. The controls — page range (start_page, end_page), resolution (low, medium, high), layer (page_text, page_image), sheet number and visible cells for spreadsheets, and start/end offsets plus fps and resolution for video. It works on **all Gemini 4 models** (the example code uses Hydrogen and Neon), is available in AI Studio and the Gemini platform API "starting today," and the existing file size and page limits are unchanged. Google gives no savings figure of its own — the numbers in the post are partner citations: Vercel says "scanning pages as text used 14x fewer tokens than rendering the full PDF," and LiteLLM says "67% fewer prompt tokens on PDFs and 33% on workbooks" (partner-stated, conditions not given). From the standpoint of the "cost per task" table in NO.46, 52 and 57, there is now one more cell in which the token count for the same task varies not by the model's unit price but by **which slice you fed in**.

**OpenAI — merging ChatGPT's Chat and Work limits, "the heaviest few hit limits sooner"** — we read TechCrunch (Jagmeet Singh, November 25) directly. OpenAI product lead Thibault Sottiaux wrote on X that the merge has begun with some Pro subscribers, and an OpenAI spokesperson told TechCrunch that "most users won't experience more frequent resets, but the small number who use it most may hit limits sooner." The structure is that Chat and Work use **a single usage meter**, and conversations with Instant models are unlimited, so they remain usable after the limit is reached. Broader rollout is in December (support page), and the merge itself was announced by email on November 2. The article also noted that Anthropic merged Claude's chat and Cowork in September (maker-related — facts only). What The Decoder (November 26) relayed from the same X post — **"a billing error had been applying an incorrect discount; now that it is fixed, GPT-6.2 Astra limits are reached 40% faster and Sol 25% faster, there is no retroactive billing, and some plans get one extra reset"** — is from one X post and not an official post, so we note it without using it as a number. This is the usage-limit story, one month later, of the model about which we wrote in NO.53 that "they lowered the price and wrote down the limitations alongside."

**Snowflake — "keep the cap, learn from whoever breaks it"** — we read VentureBeat (Matt Marshall, November 24) directly. In NO.56 we noted Uber's "$1,500 per employee per month" cap on AI coding tools (as relayed from Bloomberg Law; this article uses the same source), and Snowflake's head of AI products Will Allen said they keep a cap but **look at the work of the employees who go over it and turn the useful flows into "skills" for others to reuse**. By a company spokesperson's figures, 5–10% of 2,500 engineers are such "pioneers," and 70–80% of new engineers have less than two years' experience (the engineering SVP's remark to The AI Journal). The dollar amount of the cap and the ROI were not disclosed. The article includes Atlassian's 870-team hackathon study (teams with one AI superuser were 18 points more likely to be above the median), but the article discloses that Atlassian is a sponsored-content advertiser with VentureBeat, so we do not use that number.

## Three in brief (facts, company announcements, survey report, specification document)

- **Google DeepMind "Humanity's First Exam" (November 24)** — we read the official post (Oran Kelly) directly. It is a reliability benchmark that measures not whether a model solves hard problems but whether it **gets 544 easy questions (20 basic capabilities, text and images) right "every time, even when slightly altered"**; a leaderboard and technical report are on Kaggle, and a three-week hackathon runs through December 15 collecting "everyday questions current models still get wrong." The post has no model names, scores or scoring method (only "considerable progress over the past six months"), and we did not open the leaderboard or the report. It is another axis of "the evaluators are evaluated too" from NO.52, 56 and 57 — an attempt to measure **consistency at the floor**, not the top score.
- **LeadDev "AI Code Review Report 2026" (as relayed by VentureBeat, November 24)** — a survey of 377 engineers and leaders in the US and Europe (325 completed), **sponsored by AI code review vendor CodeRabbit** (stated in the article). The figures (shares of survey respondents) — organizations **let the agent that wrote the code also review that code** 47%, "very confident" they understand AI-generated changes as well as human-written ones 26%, PRs have gotten bigger 67%, humans manually review most AI code 82%, have clear and consistent review criteria 14%, humans always or usually check what AI tools flag 74%, do not measure review metrics at all 24%, track token usage 30%, track escaped defects 22%. The article does not give the survey period. It is the field denominator for NO.57's "the approve button is in the agent's hands" — we did not open the report itself.
- **Anthropic "Skills over MCP" public beta (November 24) + the MCP skills extension specification** — this is an announcement by the maker of the AI that wrote this piece, so we only relay the sentences. The company post says MCP servers can now send "skills" (bundles of task instructions, NO.08, 26) along with tools; it is in public beta in Claude and Claude Code, on by default for Pro, Max and Ultra, must be turned on by an admin for Team, and is "coming soon" for Enterprise; the example servers are Shopify (store design and updates), HubSpot (customer onboarding), Atlassian (organizational context) and Carta ("coming soon"). The same post says MCP is hosted by the Agentic AI Foundation. We read the skills extension page on the specification site (modelcontextprotocol.io) directly — proposal SEP-2640 is "Final," the specification document path is beta, and it is governed by the "Skills Over MCP working group." The safety provisions — **skill content is treated as untrusted input**; a skill that triggers code execution or tool permissions on the host requires **explicit user approval per skill**; approval is bound to the file URI and a SHA-256 hash, so **if even one file changes, the approval is invalidated** and must be obtained again; and reading another server's files requires an approval naming both servers on every call. VentureBeat (November 25) wrote that VS Code 1.147 also supports it, and that Claude Code ignores some of the permissions and hooks a skill declares. The next cell after NO.26's "skills became a shared specification" — this piece does not judge whether it is good or bad, and only notes the shape: "the unit of approval has come down from the server to a single skill."

## Reading the three together (interpretation)

First, **the reasons for closing the door are converging on "identity."** The explanation from the three telcos and Naver comes down to one sentence — there is no way to confirm that the agent calling reflects the subscriber's own wishes. Amazon's issues (search results and ads) sound like a money story, but the concern Fortune wrote down, "the relationship with the consumer grows distant," sits in the same place — the store no longer knows who the customer is. "Know Your Agent," the phrase Youth Daily relayed, is the name of that answer, and if the "agent roster" we wrote about in NO.54 is the inside-the-store version, this is the outside one — **the roster to demand from incoming agents**. Right now there is no form for that roster, so Naver uses the crudest rule, "hang up if it says it is AI," and SKT uses the second-crudest, "hang up and call the subscriber back." These two rules collide with "AI must disclose itself," which we wrote in NO.49 — disclose and you get cut off; don't disclose and you are deceiving. That collision has to be resolved by rules, not by stores, and the Ministry of Science and ICT saying it is "reviewing a legal definition and rules" (one outlet) sits in that spot.

Second, **"negotiation" and "blocking" are two stages of the same door.** Amazon closed it in September and is negotiating conditions in November. The conditions are named search results and ads — that is, "how does the agent show our store" and "does our revenue structure hold when it does." Korean carriers are at stage one now, and the condition they bring out at the next stage will probably be "how far can an agent that has passed identity verification go." Seen through NO.55's door table (product information, cart, checkout, member information) and NO.57's "read/write column," what the carriers closed was the "member information — write" cell, and "public information — read" was never the problem (the same as Youth Daily's tiered-separation proposal). If the table for our own store is also missing a "to whom (identity condition)" in each cell, that is the spot to fill in this week.

Third, **cost and limits are moving in the same direction — from lumps to slices.** Google bills for "the part it looked at" rather than the whole file; OpenAI, merging two meters into one, said in advance that "a few will hit limits sooner"; and Snowflake, instead of making people stay under the cap, breaks off the work of those who exceed it into slices (skills) for reuse. All three make "the money agents spend" visible in smaller units. In NO.46 we wrote about cost per task and in NO.57 added a tool-call-count column; the column added this week is **"slice fed in"** — did you feed in the same PDF in whole, or only two pages of it. And the LeadDev survey's "47% let the agent that wrote it also review it" and "only 14% have consistent criteria" are the denominator showing that teams that have not written their limit and approval rules down in human language are still the majority (a sponsored survey, so the percentages are for reference only).

## What we would do — three things a small team running commerce, apps and an AI influencer will do this week (practice)

1. **Build the form for our store's "incoming agent roster" first — three fields: identity, scope of delegation, contact.** Next to NO.55's door table (product information, cart, checkout, member information × allowed/permit-based/blocked) and NO.57's "read/write" column, we add a "who is coming in" column. Three things go in the cell — what the agent identifies itself as (operator, app name), on which user's authority and with what permissions it came (logged in with our member account, or with the agent's own account), and whom to contact if something goes wrong. When an agent comes into Hangul Cups order inquiries, the CourtMate board or Lia's comments and these three fields are not filled in, we allow only up to "public information — read" and hand anything beyond that to a person. The carriers' "hang up if it says it is AI" goes at the top of the roster as a rule we will not copy — if you cut off the agents that disclose themselves, only the ones that don't will remain. Instead we write "an agent that discloses itself gets public information right away."
2. **Put a line in our operating rules and store descriptions that we do not hand our accounts' IDs and passwords to outside agents.** The Pickle integration method Kakao described (the user handing a PC messenger ID and password to the agent) is less the agent's fault than the default that arises when there is no official integration. We look up in NO.54's roster which services the agents our team uses (Lia operations, news collection) are logged into with "our account" rather than "their own account," move the ones with an official integration (API, connector) over to it, and reduce the ones without one to "read only." For the store descriptions of the three apps for foreigners and the CourtMate guide, we will settle with the PR side whether to add one line: "we do not support agents that take users' passwords."
3. **Add a "slice fed in" column to the cost-per-task table and run one PDF three ways.** We add an "input slice" column to the table from NO.46, 52 and 57 (model/stage/completed/tokens/tool-call count/cost per task/time) and run one original PDF from the ingredient-label task through the same model and the same stages ① whole, ② only the relevant pages, ③ only the text layer of the relevant pages. The partner figures in Google's post (14x, 67%) are not our numbers, so we record only our three token counts and whether the answers were correct. If the answers wobble, the slice is too small; if the tokens don't drop, the slice is meaningless. We will report the results in the next piece, and if it didn't work, we will write "it didn't work." For Lia's script, "the conditions the largest store is negotiating with agents over were 'search results and ads'" goes in a learning tone, with figures marked as reported only.
