NO. 55 · Practice

Agents have reached the checkout button — Google opened grocery checkout inside Search, Booking Holdings' agent wrote that "at suppliers that don't allow it, it hands the checkout page to the customer," Sabre said "customers won't page through results, they'll look at one or two" and plugged its transaction engine into Grok first, and a hotel CEO said "agents don't impulse-buy" and vowed to close the door — what a store has to decide this week is one of four kinds of door

AI agents · Agentic commerce · Google Connected Apps · Universal Commerce Protocol · Booking Holdings Vayo · Checkout handback · Sabre MCP · Grok · United Airlines · Upsell · Agent-channel pricing · Product information · 2026.11.23 · AP Holdings Editorial · 24 min read

Translated from the Korean original by AI; native-speaker review pending.

On November 20, Google announced that in the US, Connected Apps in Search and the Gemini app can build grocery orders at Albertsons, Safeway, Shaw's and Target and complete checkout (built on the Universal Commerce Protocol; no prices or figures). On November 17, Booking Holdings' AI travel agent Vayo wrote in its press release that it "books through a direct integration where one exists, otherwise navigates the booking site to make the reservation, and where a supplier does not allow agent bookings, confirms the price and hands the checkout page to the traveler" (private beta). On November 16, the travel distribution company Sabre put its first travel plugin on the Grok Bot marketplace and announced "more than 80 partners piloting or running MCP services," and its CEO told the press that "customers won't page through results; they'll look at one or two, at most three." On the other side, a hotel chain CEO said "people are swayed by promotions, agents are not" and that he would block agent bookings at his hotels, and Skift reported Booking Holdings saying LLM-channel bookings are "well under 1%." We bundle the four as "kinds of door, handles, a shelf where only one or two are visible, and numbers still small," and write the one page (a door table) a small team running commerce, apps and an AI influencer would decide this week. This is the practice edition of the "agent doors" thread in NO.45, 47, 50 and 53.

We state our interests first — the subject of this article, "the store-side decision," is our own business. Item 1, the Google news, is an announcement by a company whose models we use in testing, and the list of platforms Sabre named to the press as its 2027 expansion targets in item 3 includes Claude, made by Anthropic, the maker of the AI that wrote this article (the press release does not name it). So we add no evaluation of any company and carry over only the published numbers and sentences; figures a company announced are marked as "company-reported," and anything that appeared in only one place is noted together with its source. In NO.45 we wrote about the day United Airlines put "the customer's agent" outside the door while OpenAI let stores into ChatGPT; in NO.47, about OpenAI handing agents a wallet; in NO.50, about Shopify stores beginning to auto-publish a one-page "what the agent may do"; and in NO.53, about Meta's agent going all the way to checkout in Canadian grocery shopping. This week's four items show what shape those doors are hardening into — Google opened a checkout door inside Search, Booking Holdings' agent wrote down a middle door, "at stores that don't allow it, hand the checkout page to the customer," Sabre is trying to claim the "transaction engine" seat between stores and agents, and a hotel chain gives its reasons for closing the door in numbers. We split three company announcements (Google, Vayo, Sabre) and press reports and columns (Skift, PhocusWire) into facts, interpretation, and "if it were us."

1. Google's "Get your year-end tasks done with Connected Apps" — grocery checkout inside Search (Facts — company announcement)

We read Google's official blog post "Get your year-end tasks done with new Connected Apps" (November 20, no author listed) directly. The announcement is that in the US, Connected Apps in Search and the Gemini app can build grocery orders at Albertsons, Safeway, Shaw's and Target and complete checkout. The post says these grocery apps are "built on the Universal Commerce Protocol (UCP)" and links to a separate commerce post. In finance, Experian is already in the Gemini app and now extends to Search, for checking credit scores and comparing personal loans; in tax, H&R Block can be called up in Search for tax calculations and refund estimates; and Angi and Thumbtack (local repair and decorating help) will connect to Search "in the coming weeks." There are no prices, fees, usage figures, exact launch dates or schedules outside the US in the post. It is an announcement with no numbers at all.

We read two pieces of background alongside it. First, the October 28 Google developer post "Build and manage Connected Apps in one place" (bylined by two product leads for Search and the Gemini app). It opened the Connected Apps Platform in preview; developers integrate via a cloud MCP server or Android MCP (AppFunctions API), register, test and submit in the Google Connected Console, choose where to appear across Search and the Gemini app, and after launch manage traffic, latency, error rates and verified logos and accounts. It says "more than 50 Connected Apps" are already out (productivity, ticketing, health, fitness), and that Adobe, Experian, Localiza and Peloton are in Gemini and coming to Search "in the coming weeks." This post has no UCP, fees, review timelines or country list. Second, Target's official fact sheet "How Target is Shaping Shopping Experiences in Conversational AI" (updated June 18). Target describes itself as the first major retailer with checkout on three platforms — Google Search (including AI Mode) and the Gemini app, Microsoft Copilot, and OpenAI's ChatGPT — and describes the Google flow as "question → options → "Buy" → sign in to Target account → confirm," with payment by credit card through Google Pay or a Target Circle card, and "checkout of multiple items at once is in the works" (as of June). One number — "AI-driven traffic rose 2,000% year over year in Q1" (company-reported by Target; retail overall about 400%, citing Adobe data). From the first round of Connected Apps in July (Instacart, Canva, YouTube Music; US, opt-in, purchases require user approval — eWeek, July 17) to this November's grocery checkout is four months.

2. Booking Holdings' Vayo "books the entire trip" — integration, browsing, and "handing the checkout page to the customer" (Facts — company announcement and press)

We read the press release from Vayo, the AI travel startup under Booking Holdings (November 17, Seattle, PR Newswire — we read the finviz reprint, which carries the original URL), and the PhocusWire and Skift pieces (Skift's tech reporter Adriana Lee, November 17). The announcement is that one month after launch, Vayo books flights, hotels, vacation rentals, restaurants, activities, event tickets, rental cars, trains, buses, ferries, airport transfers and parking (available to private-beta members the same day; there is a waitlist).

The method is the heart of this article. The press release says "where a direct integration exists, it books through the integration; where none exists, it navigates the booking site and completes the reservation on the traveler's behalf," and that "if a supplier does not support agent bookings, Vayo finds the best option, confirms the price, and hands the traveler a checkout page prepared so they can finish right away." Every booking requires traveler approval, and a booking is marked "confirmed" only after the supplier's confirmation. Sign-in goes through 1Password for Agents, without viewing or storing passwords, and payment through Stripe's Link agent wallet, without seeing payment details. Pricing — when Booking.com is the best option, every Vayo traveler gets Booking.com's top-tier Genius Level 3 pricing (up to 20% off at some properties). Vayo's own fees, its supplier list and booking counts are not in the press release. PhocusWire quoted Vayo CEO Mike Fridgen as saying "where a supplier does not allow it, as with United, we hand over the checkout page, and we welcome the chance to work together through permitted channels," and wrote that Booking Holdings disclosed at launch a "plan to invest $100 million, conditional on product progress and market conditions," and announced the same amount for New York-based Lola (founded by KAYAK's founders) (the $100 million is from two outlets, PhocusWire and Skift, and is not in the press release). Skift wrote that the direct integrations are Booking.com (hotels, rental cars, activities), OpenTable and SeatGeek, while flights, trains and the like go through browsing permitted supplier sites; it noted that Genius pricing could tilt comparisons toward Booking's own inventory (Skift's analysis), and added the numbers that in Skift Research's October survey only 27% of US travelers said they were comfortable booking with AI, and AI's share was 21% at the "inspiration" stage versus 6% at the "booking" stage. The company has not disclosed booking counts or the share of bookings handed off at checkout.

3. Sabre's "transaction engine" — first travel plugin on Grok Bot, "more than 80 MCP partners," "customers only look at one or two" (Facts — company announcement and press)

We read the press release from Sabre, the travel distribution (GDS) company (November 16, Southlake, Texas, PR Newswire — finviz reprint), along with PhocusWire and Skift (November 19, same reporter). At its investor day in New York on November 17, Sabre previewed the "first global travel plugin" to go on the Grok Bot marketplace and announced that its conversational booking and servicing interface Mosaic Travel Connector is "coming soon in beta." It sits on top of the MCP server and agent APIs released in 2025, and the release says "more than 80 partners are piloting or running MCP services" (company-reported; no split between pilot and production and no names). The newly announced "event-driven APIs" watch for cancellation and delay signals, identify affected travelers, and push alerts into AI tools connected to agency systems; responses take three paths — a human agent; the connector surfacing policy-compliant options inside the conversation for one-tap approval; or an autonomous agent handling it directly. Price monitoring is in beta, the plan is "expansion to major conversational models and agency workflows during 2027," and fees, revenue terms and commissions are not in the press release. Nor does the press release name any platform other than Grok.

What the press added. PhocusWire reported that Sabre CEO Kurt Ekert said on the Q3 earnings call that "LLM platforms are still more focused on the enterprise opportunity than the consumer one," and Skift quoted Ekert saying "consumers will not page through results; they will get one, two, at most three options," and listed ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini as 2027 expansion targets (these three names are Skift's reporting, not in the press release). Skift's summary includes Ekert's view that "the incentives now paid to travel agencies could shift into revenue sharing with LLMs" and Skift's assessment that "the economics are unproven and largely absent from the multi-year outlook"; since this part sits in the article's summary block rather than the body, we mark it Skift summary, one source only.

4. The other side — "agents don't impulse-buy" and "LLM-channel bookings are well under 1%" (Facts — press column and company remarks)

We read Skift founder Rafat Ali's column "The AI Agent Is Travel's Ad Blocker" (November 18) — the first five paragraphs are open and the rest is behind a paywall, so we read the remainder only through the article's summary block. The gist of the open text: travel companies' profits come from impulse purchases at checkout (a better seat, a room upgrade), and an agent booking on the traveler's behalf does not impulse-buy — so upsell profits are at risk. Tyler Morse, CEO of the hotel chain MCR, voiced this worry at the Skift Commerce Summit earlier this month, and according to the summary block he said "people are swayed by promotions, agents are not," and that he wants to block agent bookings at his hotels. A November 15 report from the analyst firm Citizens tested bookings with two agents (Instinct and Meta's Muse), found them "not impressive," yet saw a large long-term risk to online travel agencies, ranking exposure as Expedia, then Booking Holdings, then Airbnb (least) (as relayed by Skift; method not confirmed).

Numbers that appear only in the summary block we mark as Skift summary, one source only — airline ancillary revenue of $157 billion in 2025, about 16% of the total (versus $67.4 billion and 9.1% in 2016, IdeaWorksCompany estimates); Expedia's Q3 advertising revenue of $206 million; the Airbnb CEO's remark about a "$1 billion advertising opportunity"; Instinct's answer when Skift asked whether ad placement affects its recommendations — "placement is not a signal I use"; Booking Holdings saying LLM-channel bookings are "well under 1%" of total room nights; and Wyndham saying its AI phone agent books at rates averaging 15% above published rates (cited in the column as an example that "agents can sell too"). The summary also notes that United's agent restriction (NO.45) was announced the same day, and that its stated reason was "security and regulatory risk," not revenue.

Two short ones (Facts — company announcements)

  • Google's "move your history from other AI apps into Gemini" (November 19) — five steps: copy the prompt from Gemini's import settings → paste it into the previous AI tool to get a summary of your preferences → paste that answer into Gemini → export your conversation history as a ZIP from the previous provider's account → upload it in Gemini settings. Which providers are supported, size limits, plan requirements and retention terms are not in the post. It is a "switching door" — the consumer-side version of NO.48's "swapping a model takes weeks."
  • Google Gemini API "Tool Retrieval" (November 19, preview) — when an agent carries dozens or hundreds of tools, instead of sending every tool's parameter spec each time, it keeps only names and descriptions and fetches the spec when needed (a `tool_search` tool plus `defer_loading` on tools to hold back). An entire remote MCP server can also be lazy-loaded; there is no separate charge (fetched specs are then billed as input tokens), and there are no savings figures in the post, which says to "measure on your own traffic." It sits in the same place as item 1's Connected Apps connecting over MCP — the technology for when the "tools" a store exposes multiply.

Reading the four together (Interpretation)

First, the door has split into four — direct integration, browsing allowed, checkout handback, and blocked. Between the two we described in NO.45 as "block the customer's agent, let the stores in," Vayo wrote down two middle doors. "Browsing allowed" means the agent moves through the store's site like a person and completes the booking; "checkout handback" means the agent does everything up to confirming the price and the customer presses only the final checkout button. What matters is that the agent reaches our checkout page even through the "checkout handback" door — even if a store decides nothing, an agent can read the site, fill the cart and stand the customer in front of the checkout page. So there is no option called "our store has nothing to do with agents"; all that remains is to decide which of the four doors. We also note that United (security and regulation) and MCR (upsell) chose "blocked" for different reasons — even blocking comes with two kinds of reasons.

Second, the handle is a standard, and a small store is not the one choosing the standard but the one following it. Google's grocery checkout sits on UCP, the Connected Apps platform on MCP, Sabre's transaction engine also on MCP, and Shopify stores on poppy.json (NO.50). Three standards appear to be competing, but seen from the store's side the common ground is larger — publish products, prices, inventory and terms in a machine-readable format, write down "what may be done," and let a wallet (Stripe Link, Google Pay) carry the payment. Sabre's "more than 80" is a number that does not separate pilots from production (company-reported), and Google's "more than 50" is a count of apps, not transaction volume. We cannot decide how many standards survive, but getting one page of product information exactly right is needed whichever standard wins — NO.53's "one page of product information for agents to read" is this week's cheapest preparation.

Third, "only one or two are visible" changes the shelf. Ekert's "consumers will not page through; they will get one, two, three options" means the search-ad era's competition for "top of the ten results" becomes a competition over "are we inside the agent's selection criteria." And Vayo's Genius Level 3 shows a new column called "agent-channel pricing" — the same room can cost one price when a person buys it on the site and another when an agent buys it for them, and Skift also pointed out that the difference can influence which inventory gets chosen. Rafat Ali's "ad blocker" point is the other side — agents don't buy upsells, so the "one more offer" revenue at checkout may shrink. Wyndham's "the AI phone agent sells 15% higher" (Skift summary, one source only) is the counterexample the column cites that a store-side agent can sell. Taken together, a store must decide two things: the price and terms to show the customer's agent, and what our own agent will recommend.

Fourth, the numbers are still small — which is why deciding now is cheap. Booking Holdings' "well under 1%" (Skift summary, one source only), Skift Research's "6% at the booking stage," Ekert's "LLM platforms are still on the enterprise side," Citizens' "not impressive," plus Airbnb's "negligible" in NO.43 and Uber's "four rides in its busiest week" in NO.40 — by every company's numbers, the share of customer-side agents that actually reach checkout is still in the single digits or lower. Google published no figures at all. So this week's task is not integration development but writing down the kind of door — it costs nothing, and it is far cheaper than fixing things after agents multiply. And if a handed-off checkout page breaks (a login wall, an expired cart, a CAPTCHA), then under Vayo's rule "confirmed" appears only after the supplier's confirmation, so a broken checkout page ends not as revenue but as "not booked."

If it were us — the one page a team running commerce, apps and an AI influencer would write this week (Practice)

  1. One door table — for each of our products and services, write one of the four. Hangeul Cubs products, CourtMate's paid features, collaboration inquiries on Lia's channel, app subscriptions — for each line, write one of direct integration / browsing allowed / checkout handback / blocked and the reason (payment and refund risk, inventory, regulation). The default is "checkout handback" — the flow where the agent builds the cart and a person presses the checkout button. Then a person walks through whether our checkout page breaks that flow: login walls, short session expiry, CAPTCHAs, whether a cart link can be shared. We borrow Vayo's two rules as they are — an order is "confirmed" only with our system's confirmation, and approval is per transaction (the same line as "sentences about money must cite a source" in NO.54, "Agent roster and incident log"). For lines marked "blocked," write whether the reason is United-style (security) or MCR-style (upsell).
  2. The handle starts with one page of product information — choose the standard later. Shopify stores already have poppy.json (NO.50), and Google's Connected Apps console and UCP are US-only, so what we can do now is information that won't trip an agent when it "confirms the price" — price, inventory, delivery date, refund terms and size specs in one place, machine-readable (structured data, a product feed), and no different prices on two of our sites. This is why Vayo wrote that it hands over the checkout page "after confirming the price" — a mismatch ends as "not chosen" by the agent. We read Sabre's "80" and Google's "50" not as grounds for our decision but only as a signal that "three or more standards are in circulation."
  3. Two lines of terms to get onto the "shelf where only one or two are visible," and one line on agent-channel pricing. For our product to make it in when an agent narrows options to one or two, comparable terms (delivery date, refunds, total price) must be written down — we add those two lines to every Hangeul Cubs set and every basketball-shoe recommendation post. And like Genius Level 3, we decide in one line whether agent-channel orders get different prices or terms (our answer starts as "same price, no upsell" — as Rafat Ali's point goes, the "one more" at checkout doesn't work on agents, so instead of upselling we make the set composition itself the product). In Lia's scripts, the tone is learning about "the era when agents choose on the customer's behalf," and figures go in only as "reported." On our own AI-usage side, we walk through the "switching door" once — we try exporting our conversation history, and if there is knowledge that can't be exported, we move it into documents outside the model, per NO.48's principle.
