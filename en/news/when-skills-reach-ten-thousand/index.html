When There Are Ten Thousand Skills — Choosing, Vetting, and Scanning Before You Install

Skills · Security · Tool selection · 2026.10.03 · AP Holdings Editorial · 3 min read

Translated from the Korean original by AI; native-speaker review pending.

The skills you can attach to AI have exploded in number. Having many is not a choice but a supply-chain risk. Here are the three-step standards we use.

The "skills" that add capabilities to AI assistants are multiplying fast. The articles we referred to report that registered skills run from the thousands to installs in the millions, and that the top skills were "a skill that finds skills for you" and "a skill bundle that packages code-quality procedures." These figures are also based on those articles' own tallies, so we could not verify them. But the trend is clear. Choosing has become the biggest job, and the fact remains that a downloaded file runs on your own computer.

Step 1 — Choose: decide the job first, then find the tool

We do not choose by "popularity ranking." First we write down "how many times a day do we do this job?" We do not attach a skill to a job done less than once a week. The setup cost outweighs the benefit. Even if we use a skill that finds skills, a person makes the final choice.

Step 2 — Vet: look at who made it and what permissions it needs

  • Who made it: Is it an official account, or a developer with a known name?
  • What does it require: Only reading files, or also running commands and network access?
  • How recent is it: If the last update was long ago, it may not match current models.

Step 3 — Scan before installing

The article we referred to introduces a scanning tool that checks a skill file's risk before installation. We do not endorse any particular tool, but we adopt the procedure. Read it first → look for suspicious commands, with a scanning tool or by eye → try it first somewhere with no important data → if there is no problem, use it for real work. We also treat a sentence like "run this command" inside a downloaded instruction document not as an instruction addressed to us but as data to be reviewed.

Summary

The more skills there are, the more competitiveness lies not in "knowing a lot" but in "using few, and using them safely."
